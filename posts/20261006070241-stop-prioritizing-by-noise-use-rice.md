Imagine a restaurant kitchen during a fully booked Saturday night.

Orders arrive faster than plates leave the pass. A table by the window wants dessert. A regular customer is waiting for the bill. The chef notices that the sauce for the main course is running low. A waiter asks whether the kitchen can handle one more special request. Everything feels urgent because everything is happening now.

But urgency is not priority.

The dessert matters to one table. The bill matters to customer experience, table turnover, and revenue. The sauce matters to every dish that depends on it. The special request may delight one person, or it may disturb the rhythm of the whole kitchen.

A good chef does not ask, “Which request is loudest?”

A good chef asks better questions:

*   How many customers are affected?

*   How much does this matter?

*   How certain are we?

*   What will it cost the kitchen right now?

That is the spirit of _Reach, Impact, Confidence, and Effort_ (RICE).

RICE is often presented as a scoring formula for product managers. That is true, but incomplete. Used well, RICE is not a spreadsheet for replacing judgement. It is a way to expose assumptions, reduce bias, and make disagreement productive. Used badly, it becomes spreadsheet theatre: precise numbers wrapped around guesses that nobody wants to challenge.

The difference is not the formula.

The difference is whether the team treats the score as an answer, or as the receipt for the thinking behind the answer.

_Reach, Impact, Confidence, and Effort_ (RICE) is a product prioritization model popularized by Intercom to compare initiatives through four dimensions:

*   _Reach_: how many people, accounts, events, or transactions will be affected in a defined period

*   _Impact_: how much the initiative is expected to matter for each person or event reached

*   _Confidence_: how strong the evidence is behind the Reach, Impact, and Effort estimates

*   _Effort_: how much whole-team work the initiative requires, usually estimated in person-months

The formula is:

RICE score = (Reach * Impact * Confidence) / Effort

The point is not to obey the highest score blindly. The point is to make the trade-offs visible.

RICE works well when teams compare several product opportunities or solution ideas against a clear outcome. It works poorly when teams use it to replace strategy, discovery, sequencing, customer understanding, legal constraints, ethical judgement, or risk management.

RICE answers one question well:

`Given what we currently believe, which option appears to offer the highest expected impact per unit of effort?`
It does not answer these questions by itself:

*   Is this strategically important?

*   Is this legally required?

*   Is this ethically necessary?

*   Is this a dependency?

*   Is this table-stakes work?

*   Have we understood the real customer problem?

*   Are we optimizing a metric while damaging the product?

A good RICE conversation should end with a decision, but it should also leave behind a trail of assumptions that can be revisited when the team learns more.

Most teams do not struggle with prioritization because they lack ideas.

They struggle because they have too many plausible ideas.

A support lead wants to reduce tickets. Sales wants one missing feature to unblock enterprise deals. Product wants activation to improve. Engineering wants to remove a dangerous piece of infrastructure. Leadership wants something visible before the next board meeting. Design wants to fix a confusing experience that keeps showing up in research.

None of these requests are automatically foolish.

That is what makes prioritization hard.

Weak prioritization conversations often sound like this:

*   “This is really important.”

*   “Customers are asking for it.”

*   “Sales needs it.”

*   “It should not take long.”

*   “We promised it.”

*   “The competitor already has it.”

*   “This will improve retention.”

*   “This is strategic.”

Those sentences may be true, but they are incomplete. They hide the real variables.

How many customers? Which customers? How much retention? How strong is the evidence? What is the opportunity cost? What else will not happen if we do this?

When those assumptions stay hidden, prioritization becomes a contest of volume, seniority, charisma, fear, and recency.

The loudest pain wins. The most recent escalation wins. The highest-ranking stakeholder wins. The customer with the biggest contract wins. The idea with the best story wins.

RICE gives the team a slower, calmer room. It asks people to put their assumptions on the table. Not because the table creates truth, but because visible assumptions can be challenged, improved, and compared.

That is the first important shift:

RICE is not a machine for discovering the right answer. It is a conversation structure for making weak assumptions easier to see.

RICE was introduced by Intercom as a way to compare product ideas using a consistent set of factors: Reach, Impact, Confidence, and Effort. Intercom defines Reach as the number of people or events affected in a time period, Impact as the effect on each person, Confidence as the strength of the evidence behind the estimates, and Effort as the total team work required.

RICE tries to solve four common prioritization failures.

First, teams overvalue ideas that feel personally interesting. A feature that the team would love to use may not reach many customers. Reach asks who is affected, not who is enthusiastic.

Second, teams confuse activity with impact. A feature may be elegant, impressive, or technically satisfying without changing the outcome that matters. Impact asks whether the work moves a meaningful behavior, result, or risk.

Third, teams treat weak evidence as if it were strong evidence. Confidence forces the team to distinguish measured behavior, research-backed insight, anecdote, executive belief, and hope.

Fourth, teams underestimate cost. Effort makes opportunity cost explicit. Every initiative consumes capacity that cannot be used elsewhere.

The formula is useful. The discipline behind the formula is more useful.

RICE is a way of saying:

Do not just tell me what you want.

Tell me who it reaches, why it matters, how sure you are, and what it costs.

RICE looks simple because it has only four variables. The difficulty is not remembering them. The difficulty is scoring them without lying to yourself.

Reach is the number of people, accounts, users, customers, transactions, sessions, orders, teams, or events that an initiative will affect within a defined time period.

The time period matters.

“10,000 users” is not precise enough. Is that per week, per month, per quarter, or once in the lifetime of the product? A one-time migration affecting 10,000 users is not the same kind of reach as a recurring workflow improvement affecting 10,000 users every month.

A good Reach definition has three parts:

*   A population

*   A unit

*   A timeframe

Reach should be as real as possible. Intercom recommends using product metrics where possible rather than inventing numbers. That matters because Reach is where personal bias often enters quietly. Teams naturally notice the users they speak to most, the accounts that escalate most, and the workflows they personally understand best.

Good Reach estimation does not remove judgement, but it makes judgement inspectable.

Impact asks how much the initiative matters for each person, account, or event reached.

Reach asks, “How many?”

Impact asks, “How much?”

This is the most delicate part of RICE because product impact is rarely easy to isolate. ProductPlan makes the right warning here: even after collecting data, it can be hard to prove that one initiative was the main reason users changed behavior. Estimating that impact before building is harder.

Intercom uses a rough scale:

![Image 2](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!4HHO!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdfdc0db6-f6c5-478a-8936-e46b9d128219_313x227.png)

This scale is intentionally rough. It does not pretend to be scientific precision. It gives the team enough structure to compare ideas without spending the whole session arguing whether something is a 1.7 or a 1.8.

Impact must be defined relative to the outcome you care about.

If the product outcome is activation, Impact might mean the expected change in successful onboarding.

If the outcome is retention, Impact might mean the expected reduction in churn risk.

If the outcome is operational reliability, Impact might mean the expected reduction in incidents, manual intervention, or recovery time.

If the outcome is support efficiency, Impact might mean the expected reduction in avoidable tickets.

Impact becomes dangerous when it floats free from the outcome. “This is high impact” is vague. “This is high impact because it directly affects the largest drop-off in our activation funnel” is useful.

A good Impact conversation asks:

*   Which outcome does this initiative support?

*   What user behavior do we expect to change?

*   What business result do we expect that behavior to influence?

*   What evidence suggests this change will happen?

*   What would make us admit later that the impact was lower than expected?

*   What guardrail metric could warn us that we improved the target number while damaging the product?

Impact is where hope likes to dress as strategy.

RICE does not remove that temptation, but it gives the team a place to challenge it.

There is another discipline hidden inside Impact: the team should define what it refuses to break.

If the goal is activation, a guardrail might be support tickets, refund requests, or early churn. If the goal is engagement, a guardrail might be task completion, user trust, or notification opt-outs. If the goal is revenue, a guardrail might be customer complaints, downgrade requests, or sales promises that the product cannot honestly keep.

This matters because a metric can improve while the product gets worse. Nielsen Norman Group’s discussion of Campbell’s Law is useful here: when a metric becomes too important in decision-making, people may start optimizing the metric instead of the real outcome. A RICE score should therefore make two things visible: the value we hope to create, and the damage we are not willing to create along the way.

Confidence is the honesty factor.

It asks: how much evidence do we have behind our Reach, Impact, and Effort estimates?

Intercom uses these values:

![Image 3](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Y3lA!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F6064ad91-e4a8-4c53-9d3b-58645eb76f2f_391x194.png)

Confidence is not optimism. It is not how excited the team feels. It is not stakeholder conviction. It is not the strength of someone’s presentation.

Confidence is the quality of the evidence.

A team might have high Confidence when:

*   Reach comes from product analytics

*   Impact is supported by user research, experiments, or strong historical patterns

*   Effort has been discussed with the people who will do the work

*   Dependencies are known

*   The problem is well understood

A team might have low Confidence when:

*   Reach comes from anecdotes

*   Impact comes from a stakeholder opinion

*   Effort excludes design, research, data, migration, enablement, support, or operational work

*   The team has not spoken to affected users

*   The solution is technically unfamiliar

*   The work depends on another team that has not agreed to the plan

Confidence is one of the best parts of RICE because it creates a socially acceptable way to say, “This might be valuable, but we do not know enough yet.”

That sentence is gold.

Many organizations punish uncertainty indirectly. They reward confident slides, crisp roadmaps, and decisive language. The problem is that certainty can be performed. Evidence cannot.

Confidence helps teams avoid treating a dream with a spreadsheet as if it were a fact.

Effort is the cost side of the model.

Intercom defines Effort as the total amount of time required from all members of the team, including product, design, and engineering, estimated in person-months. It also explains why Effort divides the score: unlike Reach, Impact, and Confidence, more Effort is worse because it consumes more capacity.

Effort is often underestimated because teams silently reduce it to engineering implementation.

That is a mistake.

Real product work may include:

*   Product discovery

*   User research

*   Design exploration

*   Interaction design

*   Content design

*   Engineering implementation

*   Architecture changes

*   Data modeling

*   Migration work

*   Quality assurance and exploratory testing

*   Security review

*   Legal review

*   Accessibility review

*   Documentation

*   Internal enablement

*   Customer communication

*   Support training

*   Observability

*   Rollout planning

*   Incident preparation

*   Post-release measurement

An initiative that looks like “just add a toggle” may require permission modeling, analytics, migration, support workflows, audit logs, documentation, and rollback behavior. If the Effort estimate ignores that, the RICE score becomes fiction.

Effort should be rough, but not careless.

The point is not to spend weeks estimating. The point is to avoid pretending that only coding counts.

The RICE formula is:

RICE score = (Reach * Impact * Confidence) / Effort

Intercom describes the resulting score as total impact per time worked. In other words, RICE estimates evidence-adjusted impact divided by capacity cost.

That means the formula is not asking:

`Which idea is biggest?`
It is asking:

`Which idea appears to create the most expected impact for the effort required?`
That distinction matters.

A huge initiative can have a lower score than a smaller one if it consumes far more effort.

A small initiative can have a high score if it reaches many people, has meaningful impact, is supported by strong evidence, and is cheap to deliver.

A speculative initiative can be reduced by low Confidence even if its imagined upside is enormous.

This is the economic intuition behind RICE:

Reach * Impact = possible value

Possible value * Confidence = evidence-adjusted value

Evidence-adjusted value / Effort = value per capacity unit

A high score does not mean “do this automatically.”

A high score means “this deserves serious attention.”

A low score does not mean “never do this.”

A low score means “if we do this, we need a reason that the formula does not capture.”

That reason may be valid. Dependencies, legal requirements, strategic bets, ethical obligations, reliability problems, and table-stakes capabilities may justify doing lower-scored work first. Intercom explicitly warns that RICE scores should not be used as a rigid rule because teams may need to work out of order for reasons such as dependencies or table-stakes features.

This is the second important shift:

RICE is not the decision. RICE is a structured argument.

Imagine a _business-to-business_ (B2B) _software as a service_ (SaaS) product with an activation problem.

The team’s current outcome is:

`Increase the percentage of new accounts that complete setup within 14 days.`
The team is considering four initiatives:

1.   Guided setup checklist

2.   Import wizard improvements

3.   Admin permission templates

4.   In-app educational video library

The team agrees to score Reach per quarter.

This second table is not administrative decoration. It is the part that prevents the RICE table from becoming a costume for opinion. If the team later learns that permission complexity is the real blocker, the admin templates score should change. If engineering discovers that the import wizard requires a risky data migration, the Effort score should change. If an experiment shows that the checklist does not affect completion, the Impact score should change.

At first glance, the guided setup checklist wins.

But a good team does not stop at sorting the spreadsheet.

Why does the checklist score highest? Because it reaches many accounts, has medium expected impact, has decent evidence, and is relatively cheap.

Why does the import wizard score lower even though its impact is massive? Because it reaches fewer accounts and requires more work.

Why does admin permission templates score lower than expected? Because the team has weak Confidence. Maybe Sales is loud about this problem, but the team has not yet validated whether permissions are the actual activation blocker.

Why does the video library score lowest? Because it reaches many users, but the team has low Confidence and expects low impact. It may be useful later, but it does not appear to be the strongest activation lever now.

A weak decision sounds like this:

We are doing the checklist because it has the highest score.

A stronger decision sounds like this:

We are doing the guided setup checklist first because it has the highest evidence-adjusted impact per unit of effort against our activation outcome.

We are not rejecting import wizard improvements. We are sequencing them later because they require more effort and affect a smaller segment.

We will run discovery on admin permission templates because the score is being suppressed by weak Confidence, not necessarily by weak value.

That is a much better decision.

It explains what the team will do. It explains what the team will not do. It explains why. It explains what needs more learning.

The roadmap becomes less like a throne room and more like a lab notebook.

A RICE session should not begin with a spreadsheet.

It should begin with the outcome.

Without a clear outcome, every idea can look important. With a clear outcome, the team can ask whether each idea plausibly moves the same needle.

The decision flow should look like this.

A useful outcome is specific enough to guide trade-offs.

Weak outcomes:

*   Improve the product

*   Make customers happier

*   Increase engagement

*   Build enterprise features

Better outcomes:

*   Increase the percentage of new accounts that complete setup within 14 days

*   Reduce avoidable support tickets from the billing workflow by 30%

*   Increase weekly active use of the collaboration feature among teams with more than five users

*   Reduce failed imports for customers migrating from legacy systems

The outcome is the lens. RICE is the scoring conversation inside that lens.

Do not mix everything in the same RICE table.

RICE works better when options are comparable enough to be evaluated against the same outcome.

Good comparisons:

*   Four ways to improve onboarding completion

*   Five ways to reduce billing support tickets

*   Three solutions for improving enterprise admin setup

Bad comparison:

*   Redesign onboarding

*   Replace the deployment platform

*   Add a data deletion workflow for legal compliance

*   Enter a new market

*   Fix a production incident

Some of those items may matter deeply, but they do not belong in one naive RICE ranking.

Teams should agree on:

*   The Reach timeframe

*   The Reach unit

*   The Impact scale

*   The Confidence scale

*   What is included in Effort

*   Whether Effort includes discovery, rollout, enablement, and measurement

*   What evidence is acceptable for high, medium, or low Confidence

This prevents teams from quietly changing the rules to favor their preferred idea.

If possible, ask participants to score independently before discussing.

This reduces anchoring. When the most senior person speaks first, everyone else’s numbers bend around that first number. Silent scoring gives the team more signal.

Then discuss differences.

The disagreement is not a problem. The disagreement is the value.

If one person scores Impact as 3 and another scores it as 0.5, the team has found something important. They do not share the same mental model. That is exactly what a good prioritization process should reveal.

Every score should have a short note.

Field Score Assumption Reach 4,000 Based on accounts entering setup last quarter. Impact 1 Expected to improve completion, but not solve import failure. Confidence 80% Analytics are strong, but impact is based on research patterns rather than an experiment. Effort 2 Includes design, engineering, analytics, and rollout.

The note matters because future learning needs somewhere to land.

Without notes, the team only has numbers.

With notes, the team has memory.

After calculating scores, sort the table.

Then ask:

*   Does the ranking surprise us?

*   Which score feels too high?

*   Which score feels too low?

*   Which estimate has the weakest evidence?

*   Which option has a dependency?

*   Which option is table stakes?

*   Which option is strategically important even if the score is lower?

*   Which option should not be scored with RICE at all?

The score starts the judgement. It does not end it.

Not every RICE session should end with “build the top item.”

Sometimes the right next action is:

*   Build

*   Do discovery

*   Run an experiment

*   Split the initiative

*   Re-estimate Effort

*   Clarify the outcome

*   Remove an option

*   Escalate a strategic trade-off

*   Use a different prioritization method

A mature team can say:

This has a promising score, but Confidence is too low.

Our next step is not delivery.

Our next step is learning.

RICE is often used too late and too narrowly.

A team collects feature requests, scores them, and calls that prioritization. The problem is that a feature request is already a solution. If the team has not understood the opportunity behind it, the RICE table may compare shallow answers to poorly understood problems.

This is where Opportunity Solution Trees are useful.

Teresa Torres describes Opportunity Solution Trees as a way to visually represent paths toward a desired outcome. The tree starts with an outcome, then maps customer opportunities, then possible solutions, then assumption tests. Product Talk also emphasizes that the tree helps teams externalize and visualize their thinking so they can align around what to do next.

That structure complements RICE.

RICE can help compare solutions.

Opportunity Solution Trees help avoid comparing random solutions detached from customer opportunities.

A healthier flow looks like this.

The loop matters.

RICE is not a one-time sorting ceremony. It should change when the team learns.

If discovery shows that a problem reaches fewer users than expected, Reach should go down.

If interviews reveal that the pain is severe, Impact may go up.

If an experiment produces strong evidence, Confidence should go up.

If engineering discovers hidden complexity, Effort should go up.

The score should not be protected from reality. The score should absorb reality.

RICE works well when five conditions are present.

RICE needs a target. Not a target in the sense of metric fixation, but a direction for comparison.

If the outcome is activation, initiatives are scored according to their expected contribution to activation.

If the outcome is support reduction, initiatives are scored according to their expected contribution to support reduction.

Without an outcome, RICE becomes a generic attractiveness contest.

RICE works better for comparing several options that aim at the same kind of result.

For example:

*   Which onboarding improvement should we build first?

*   Which billing pain should we address first?

*   Which adoption lever should we try first?

*   Which integration improvement has the best evidence-adjusted return?

It works worse when the table mixes unrelated categories of work.

RICE becomes stronger when Reach can be connected to analytics, customer data, support volume, transaction data, account data, or research synthesis.

It becomes weaker when Reach is a vague feeling.

Confidence is the antidote to theatre.

A team that gives everything 100% Confidence is not using RICE. It is decorating opinions.

Effort should include more than coding. Otherwise, RICE will systematically favor work that hides costs outside engineering implementation.

RICE can mislead teams in predictable ways.

A feature that reaches many users with low impact may outrank a feature that deeply helps a smaller group.

That may be correct, but not always.

If the smaller group represents a strategic segment, a retention risk, a regulatory need, or a high-value customer type, raw Reach may understate importance.

A team should ask:

Are all reached users equally important for this outcome?

Sometimes the answer is no.

In that case, Reach may need segmentation, weighting, or a different model.

Internal platform work, architecture improvements, reliability investments, observability, security, and technical debt reduction often have indirect Reach and indirect Impact.

If the team forces these items into the same RICE table as customer-facing features, they may look weak.

That does not mean they are weak.

It may mean RICE is the wrong tool for that decision.

For example, a database migration may have low visible customer Impact this quarter but high risk reduction. A reliability improvement may not increase activation, but it may reduce incident probability and protect trust.

Use the right economic frame. For risk reduction and time-critical sequencing, _Weighted Shortest Job First_ (WSJF) or a Cost of Delay conversation may be more appropriate.

A score like 1,437.5 can look scientific.

It may still be built from fragile estimates.

The decimal does not make the assumption true.

Teams should avoid worshipping exact scores. It is often better to group results into bands:

*   Clearly high

*   Promising

*   Needs learning

*   Low priority for this outcome

*   Not suitable for this scoring model

The ranking matters less than the reasoning.

RICE can reduce bias, but it can also launder bias.

A stakeholder can inflate Impact. A team can underestimate Effort for an idea they like. A product manager can lower Confidence for an idea they dislike. A leadership team can demand that numbers be adjusted until the preferred initiative rises to the top.

The spreadsheet is not immune to politics.

It simply moves politics into the assumptions.

That is still progress, as long as the assumptions are visible and challengeable.

Metrics are useful, but they can become dangerous when teams optimize the number instead of the goal. Nielsen Norman Group’s article on Campbell’s Law warns that when organizations optimize metrics at the cost of everything else, they expose themselves to metric corruption and may harm users and business goals.

This applies directly to RICE.

If the team is rewarded for choosing the highest RICE score, the score can become the target. People may learn to inflate Reach, exaggerate Impact, overstate Confidence, and minimize Effort.

At that point, RICE stops being a thinking tool.

It becomes a performance.

A healthy organization does not ask, “Did you pick the highest score?”

It asks:

What did the score reveal, what did it miss, and why did we make this decision?

RICE is useful, but it is not the only prioritization tool. It should not be stretched to cover every decision.

Different tools answer different questions.

That is the real reason to compare them. A prioritization tool is not only a calculation style. It is a way of making a particular sacrifice visible. RICE makes evidence-adjusted impact per unit of effort visible. MoSCoW makes scope sacrifice visible inside a timebox. WSJF makes delay cost visible. Opportunity Solution Trees make the path from outcome to customer opportunity to solution visible.

When a team chooses the wrong tool, it often makes the wrong sacrifice invisible.

MoSCoW stands for Must Have, Should Have, Could Have, and Won’t Have this time. The Agile Business Consortium’s _Dynamic Systems Development Method_ (DSDM) guidance frames MoSCoW as a prioritization technique for fixed-time delivery, where Must Haves form the Minimum Usable SubseT and lower-priority items provide contingency.

The same guidance recommends keeping Must Have effort to a level where delivery confidence remains high, typically no more than 60% Must Have effort, with a sensible pool of Could Haves around 20%.

Use MoSCoW when the key question is:

What is necessary for this release, increment, or timebox to remain viable?

Use RICE when the key question is:

Which of these optional initiatives appears most valuable relative to effort?

A useful combination is:

1.   Use MoSCoW to identify non-negotiable Must Haves for a release

2.   Use RICE to sequence Should Haves and Could Haves

3.   Keep Won’t Have this time visible to avoid scope leakage

WSJF is an economic sequencing model. The _Scaled Agile Framework_ (SAFe) defines it as relative Cost of Delay divided by relative job duration and frames it as a way to sequence work for maximum economic benefit in a flow-based system. SAFe also cites Don Reinertsen’s warning that if you quantify only one thing, quantify Cost of Delay.

Use WSJF when delay has a meaningful economic cost.

For example:

*   A regulatory deadline is approaching

*   A market window is closing

*   A dependency blocks several teams

*   A risk grows over time

*   A short job unlocks significant value quickly

Use RICE when Reach, Impact, Confidence, and Effort are a better fit than Cost of Delay.

The two models are cousins. Both divide some notion of value by some notion of size or duration. But they emphasize different things.

RICE emphasizes expected impact and evidence.

WSJF emphasizes economic sequencing and delay cost.

Opportunity Solution Trees help teams map how customer opportunities connect to desired outcomes and possible solutions. Product Talk emphasizes that they support shared understanding, faster learning cycles, and clearer stakeholder communication.

Use Opportunity Solution Trees before RICE when the team is not yet sure which problems are worth solving.

Use RICE after discovery has produced plausible solution options.

Opportunity Solution Trees help you understand the space.

RICE helps you compare options within the space.

RICE does not remove human behavior from prioritization. It only gives human behavior a clearer surface.

The first trap is anchoring. If the most senior person says, “This is clearly a 3,” the room may orbit that number. Independent scoring before discussion helps because it preserves disagreement long enough for the team to learn from it.

The second trap is reverse-engineering the score. This happens when someone already knows what they want to build and adjusts Reach, Impact, Confidence, or Effort until the preferred initiative wins. The antidote is to ask for evidence notes before calculating the final score.

The third trap is negotiating numbers instead of assumptions. A team can waste twenty minutes arguing whether Impact is 1 or 2 while ignoring the real question: what user behavior would have to change for this initiative to matter? When the number is controversial, move the conversation underneath the number.

The fourth trap is treating Confidence as charisma. Confidence is not how persuasive the stakeholder sounds. It is the quality of the evidence behind the estimate. A confident voice with weak evidence should still produce a weak Confidence score.

The fifth trap is letting the session end with a ranking but no decision. A useful RICE session should say what happens next: build, learn, split, defer, escalate, or use another model. A sorted table without a next action is only a tidy postponement.

Good facilitation keeps the room honest. It makes disagreement useful, keeps evidence attached to numbers, and prevents the score from becoming a negotiation trophy.

A single table with all company ideas may look organized, but it usually mixes different decision types.

Do not compare a security fix, a legal requirement, a customer-facing experiment, a platform migration, and a growth feature as if they were equivalent.

Separate the work.

Then choose the right decision model.

If there is no outcome, Impact becomes vague.

The team starts scoring whether something feels useful rather than whether it moves a specific result.

RICE needs a destination.

Confidence is not how strongly someone wants the thing.

Confidence is evidence quality.

A senior stakeholder saying “I am confident” is not the same as product analytics, user research, experiments, or validated demand.

If design, research, migration, support, legal, enablement, or measurement are needed, they belong in Effort.

Invisible work is still work.

If the team has not understood the problem, RICE may help choose the best-looking wrong answer.

Use discovery first.

Some work must be done because it is legally required, ethically necessary, or safety-critical.

Do not ask a RICE score to carry moral responsibility.

A RICE score is a snapshot of current beliefs.

When evidence changes, the score should change.

If the spreadsheet stays frozen while reality moves, the team is no longer prioritizing. It is preserving a historical artifact.

Before using RICE, ask:

*   Do we have a clear product outcome?

*   Are the options comparable?

*   Have we defined the Reach unit and timeframe?

*   Have we agreed on the Impact scale?

*   Have we defined what counts as strong, medium, and weak Confidence?

*   Does Effort include all relevant functions, not only engineering?

*   Are mandatory, legal, safety, or table-stakes items handled separately?

*   Are dependencies visible?

*   Are strategic bets marked as strategic bets?

*   Are we writing down assumptions, not just numbers?

*   Do we know what learning would change the score?

*   Are we willing to override the score with an explicit reason?

After using RICE, ask:

*   What did the ranking reveal?

*   What surprised us?

*   Which estimates are weakest?

*   Which options need discovery rather than delivery?

*   Which option are we choosing?

*   Which option are we explicitly not choosing?

*   What trade-off are we accepting?

*   When will we revisit the decision?

A good prioritization process should leave the team with more than a sorted list.

It should leave the team with a clearer understanding of its own thinking.

Prioritization is not the act of saying yes to important things.

That is the easy part.

Prioritization is the act of saying no, not yet, or not now to things that may also matter.

That is why teams reach for frameworks. They want fairness, clarity, and a way to defend decisions. RICE helps, but only when used with humility and evidence.

Its strength is not mathematical precision. Its strength is disciplined visibility.

Reach makes the affected population visible. Impact makes the expected change visible. Confidence makes the evidence visible. Effort makes the cost visible. The score combines them, but the score is not wisdom. It is a prompt for better judgement.

The best use of RICE is not to end debate. It is to improve debate.

It helps teams move from:

I think this is important.

to:

We believe this reaches 4,000 accounts per quarter, has medium impact on activation, has 80% Confidence because analytics and research agree, and costs around two person-months.

That gives it a strong score, but we are still checking whether a lower-scored dependency must happen first.

That is a different kind of conversation.

Less theatre. Less noise. More explicit trade-offs. More visible assumptions. More honest decisions.

RICE will not save a team that lacks strategy. It will not replace discovery. It will not remove politics. It will not make uncertain work certain.

But it can do something valuable.

It can turn a room full of competing opinions into a map of assumptions the team can inspect together.

Back in the restaurant kitchen, the chef still has to choose. The dessert may wait. The bill may move first. The sauce may become urgent because every plate depends on it. The special request may be accepted, delayed, or refused. The point is not to pretend every decision is easy. The point is to stop confusing the loudest request with the most responsible trade-off.

That is where better product decisions begin.
