Title: AI is teaching product teams to optimise the wrong things

URL Source: http://emmanuelvalverderamos.substack.com/p/ai-is-teaching-product-teams-to-optimise

Final URL: https://emmanuelvalverderamos.substack.com/p/ai-is-teaching-product-teams-to-optimise

Published Time: 2026-04-30T06:13:45+00:00

Markdown Content:
**Every time I say AI on this article I’m talking about LLM and agents, and also MCP, because all of this elements ARE NOT AI. But everybody call this AI**

AI is making it cheap to produce product artefacts: user stories, reports, roadmaps, summaries, release notes, communications, prototypes, dashboards. Many teams are mistaking that for progress. It is not. Product work was never mainly the work of producing artefacts. It is the work of judgment under uncertainty: deciding what problem matters, why it matters, what return of invesment justifies action, what should happen first, and what should not be built at all. That is why the current wave of AI use in product is more revealing than it looks. When product teams automate low-leverage administrative or representational work first, they are not merely adopting a tool. They are exposing what they think the job is. And too often, they are exposing the wrong thing.

*   AI is not only making product teams faster. It is making it cheaper to generate product artefacts, and many teams are confusing that with better product work.

*   Product is not the backlog. Product work is deciding what deserves to exist, why, in what order, and with what expected return.

*   The most revealing thing about a team’s AI use is not that it automates. It is what it chooses to automate first.

*   When product teams automate user stories, backlog organisation, reporting, roadmap hygiene, and similar artefacts first, they are usually optimising low-leverage proxies rather than high-leverage decisions.

*   This is more serious in product than elsewhere because product should be one of the functions best able to distinguish ROI, opportunity cost, problem space, and solution space.

*   AI can be useful in product, but mostly when it reduces clerical drag or helps synthesis. It becomes dangerous when it substitutes for judgment, alignment, or real conversation.

*   The core failure is not technical. It is economic and conceptual.

*   The danger is not that AI writes. It is that product teams stop noticing what product work actually is.

The most dangerous thing AI is doing in product is not hallucinating.

*   It is not writing generic text.

*   It is not making mediocre PowerPoints.

*   It is not even producing bad user stories.

It is doing something subtler.

It is **making product teams feel productive while they optimise the least important parts of product work.**

That is the real problem.

Because once a team starts confusing artefact production with product progress, everything else gets easier to justify. More stories. More reports. More polished roadmaps. More documentation. More structure. More apparent motion. More internal neatness. More deliverables that look like control.

But product work was never mainly the work of producing artefacts.

**Product work is deciding what deserves to exist**.

**That means deciding what problem is worth solving, why now, with what evidence, with what expected return of invesment, with what opportunity cost, with what sequencing, with what trade-offs, and with what long-term effect on the product and the business.**

That is the work.

Everything else is secondary.

This is where the confusion starts.

A lot of companies say they have product teams when what they really have is a routing layer between stakeholders and engineers.

*   Requests come in.

*   Priorities get negotiated.

*   Requirements get written.

*   Tickets get created.

*   Engineering gets the work.

The language sounds modern. The operating model is old.

In that model, the backlog becomes the visible centre of gravity. So product slowly collapses into backlog administration. Once that happens, AI looks brilliant, because AI is very good at backlog administration.

It can organise.  
It can standardise.  
It can rewrite.  
It can summarise.  
It can structure.  
It can classify.  
It can generate acceptance criteria.  
It can produce cleaner artefacts than many humans will produce under pressure.

But that is precisely the trap.

If product is the backlog, AI looks transformative.  
If product is judgment, AI looks much narrower.

That is the first real reframing.

The problem is not whether AI can make backlog work more efficient.  
The problem is whether backlog work was ever the centre of product in the first place.

It was not.

A serious product function should be able to answer a specific class of questions.

*   What are we doing?

*   Why are we doing it?

*   What problem are we actually trying to solve?

*   How confident are we that it is a real problem?

*   Why this problem and not another?

*   What is the likely return of investment if we are right?

*   What is the cost if we are wrong?

*   What is the opportunity cost of spending time here?

*   How does this fit the strategy?

*   What should happen first?

*   What should not happen at all?

Those are not administrative questions.

They are judgment questions.

**They force product to think in terms of leverage, evidence, risk, cost, return of investment, and sequencing. They force product to distinguish signal from noise, urgency from value, noise from demand, and movement from progress.**

That is why the current AI behaviour in many product teams is so revealing.

Because when **product starts by automating user stories, backlog formatting, reports, roadmap organisation, and similar outputs, it is not only adopting a tool. It is declaring what it thinks matters.**

And too often, it is declaring the wrong thing.

This is the centre of the article.

The most revealing thing about a team’s use of AI is not that it uses AI.

It is where it points it first.

Here is the pattern many teams seem drawn to:

*   Generate release notes

*   Standardise documentation

*   Automate user stories

*   Organise backlog and roadmap

*   Automate iteration reports

*   Generate stakeholder communications

*   Automate tours and guides

*   Prototype dashboards

*   Create summaries and internal artefacts around ongoing work

Some of this may save time. Some of it may even be useful.

That is not the point.

The point is that these are usually not the highest-leverage questions in product work.

They are downstream artefacts.  
They are packaging.  
They are representation.  
They are administration.  
They are traces of decisions, not the decisions themselves.

That is why this is not just a tooling problem. It is a prioritisation problem.

The team is not merely automating.  
The team is choosing what deserves optimisation.

And that choice tells you how well it understands its own role.

A lot of product teams use the language of discovery, strategy, and prioritisation, but their behaviour tells a simpler story.

They are spending disproportionate energy in the solution space and almost none in the problem space.

Problem space is where the real product work begins.

*   What is happening?

*   To whom?

*   Why does it matter?

*   What evidence supports that view?

*   What is the unmet need?

*   What are the alternatives?

*   What happens if we do nothing?

*   What outcome are we actually trying to change?

Solution space is different.

*   How could we build it?

*   How could we package it?

*   How could we phrase it?

*   How could we plan it?

*   How could we automate it?

*   How could we represent it in the backlog?

Most of the current AI automations in product sit not only in the solution space, but in the representational layer of the solution space. They improve how work is written down, tracked, packaged, or communicated.

That is a very long way from the hardest product questions.

If a team has not understood the problem properly, faster story generation does not help.  
If a team has not thought through expected return of investment, cleaner roadmap structure does not help.  
If a team has not identified the true constraint, automated reporting does not help.

It only creates the appearance of progress.

The work feels modern.  
The thinking stays shallow.

For years, overengineering had a familiar home.

It lived in software teams.

Engineers built abstractions too early.  
They designed for scale that never arrived.  
They chose tools because the tools were exciting.  
They solved speculative future problems with very real current money.

The cost was not only extra effort. It was delayed learning, harder change, more fragility, worse trade-offs, and systems that became more elaborate than the business had earned the right to carry.

AI has not removed that failure mode.

It has exported it.

Now the same instinct appears in product. Not as premature abstraction in code, but as premature sophistication in planning and administration. Not as ornate architecture, but as ornate workflow. Not as technical gold plating, but as process gold plating.

That is why the article is not fundamentally about AI hype.

It is about overengineering changing departments.

What used to happen in code is now happening in product artefacts.

This is what makes the problem more serious.

You could look at a list of automations like backlog organisation, user story generation, reports, communications, documentation, roadmap hygiene, and dashboards and say: “Fine, they are just trying to save time.”

But that reading is too forgiving.

[![Image 7](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!0JzM!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F75f7f642-7f67-43cc-9d3f-ce6e5cbceb9c_1024x559.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!0JzM!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F75f7f642-7f67-43cc-9d3f-ce6e5cbceb9c_1024x559.png)

Because product should not only ask whether something can be automated.  
Product should ask whether it is worth automating first.

That is the economic question.

What is the expected return of investment on this optimisation?  
What is the opportunity cost of focusing here?  
What larger problem remains untouched while we polish this smaller one?  
Is this a real bottleneck or just a visible annoyance?  
If we had to apply a Pareto lens, would this be among the first places to optimise?

Usually, the honest answer is no.

Usually, these are not the places where product teams create the most value or destroy the most value. They are not where the biggest uncertainties live. They are not where the hardest trade-offs sit. They are not where the greatest commercial risk lies.

They are visible.  
They are irritating.  
They are easy to point AI at.

That is not the same as high leverage.

And when product teams fail to distinguish those things, the issue is no longer “bad AI usage.”

It is weak product judgment.

The user story example matters because it reveals the whole pattern in miniature.

A user story was never valuable because text existed in a certain format.  
It was valuable because it supported shared understanding.

It was supposed to create enough clarity to move, and enough incompleteness to force conversation. It was a social tool, not a miniature requirements document.

But many teams had already drifted away from that years ago. They turned stories into storage containers for detail. They preserved the ritual and lost the function. AI now accelerates that drift perfectly.

It generates better-looking shells faster.

This is why the issue is not that AI-generated stories are badly written. Some are written perfectly well.

The deeper issue is that the team may no longer understand what the story is for.

If the story becomes the thing itself, then the team keeps the artefact and loses the alignment.  
If the story becomes a generated requirement-shaped object, then the conversation is quietly demoted.  
If the conversation disappears, then understanding is assumed rather than created.

The template survives.  
The thinking thins out.

And because the output looks polished, fewer people notice.

A fair version of this argument has to concede something.

Many teams are genuinely drowning in clerical drag.

Their documentation is inconsistent.  
Their inputs are fragmented.  
Their reporting is repetitive.  
Their communication is messy.  
Their backlog is chaotic.  
Their internal artefacts are low quality.

In that environment, AI can absolutely help. It can standardise, summarise, cluster, draft, and clean up a lot of work that never deserved so much human time in the first place.

That is true.

But it does not rescue the broader pattern.

Because even if AI improves the baseline quality of product administration, the central question remains untouched:

Is this where product should be looking first for leverage?

Usually not.

A cleaner backlog is not the same as better product judgment.  
A better-formatted story is not the same as better prioritisation.  
A faster report is not the same as better decision-making.  
A more polished roadmap is not the same as a stronger strategy.

That is the second major reframing.

The problem is not that these automations have zero value.  
The problem is that they rarely have first-order value.

The proxy became the goal.

The alternative is not anti-AI theatre.

It is not refusing useful tools to make a philosophical point.  
It is not insisting that all good product work must be manual.  
It is not nostalgia.

The alternative is to use AI where it actually supports product judgment instead of substituting for it.

*   Use it to compress clerical drag.

*   Use it to summarise large volumes of incoming signals.

*   Use it to cluster bugs, requests, and support issues.

*   Use it to expose patterns worth investigating.

*   Use it to generate alternative framings.

*   Use it to stress-test assumptions.

*   Use it to surface missing information.

*   Use it to accelerate synthesis where human verification is still real.

*   Use it to make better conversations possible.

But do not confuse that with automating the work of product itself.

*   Product still has to decide what problem matters.

*   Product still has to judge expected return of investment.

*   Product still has to understand the difference between demand and noise.

*   Product still has to know what not to build.

*   Product still has to separate problem space from solution space.

*   Product still has to choose where attention goes.

AI can support parts of that work.

It cannot remove the need for it.

This point matters because it raises the moral weight of the critique without turning moralistic.

If finance optimises spreadsheet production, that may be annoying.  
If operations optimises templates before root causes, that may be wasteful.  
If engineering automates the wrong layer, that may introduce technical drag.

But product is different.

Product is supposed to be the function that best understands leverage.

Product should be unusually good at distinguishing:

*   Important from urgent

*   Signal from noise

*   Problem from solution

*   Expected return of investment from visible activity

*   Srategic work from administrative work

*   Decisions from their artefacts

So when product teams point AI first at low-leverage administrative proxies, the problem is more serious.

They are failing in the very area where they should be strongest.

That is the real accusation.

Not that they used AI.  
That they misunderstood what in their own job was worth optimising.

A company does not invest in product teams so they can produce more artefacts.

It invests in them so they can make better bets.

If AI helps reduce clerical burden, good.  
If it helps reveal patterns in noisy data, good. But double check it and never trust it  
If it helps teams prepare better conversations, good.  
If it helps expose assumptions earlier, good. But double check it and never trust it

But if product teams use it first to generate cleaner stories, neater reports, tidier backlogs, and more polished roadmap artefacts, while the real questions of leverage, ROI, problem selection, and sequencing remain weak, then the organisation is not becoming more strategic.

It is becoming more decorative.

And decorative productivity is still expensive.

The strongest version of the argument is not that product teams are using AI badly.

It is that AI is exposing a deeper weakness in product judgment.

Overengineering used to be a familiar software failure mode: too much complexity, too early, for too little return of investment. AI has now made that same failure mode easier to perform in product through the rapid generation of strategies, stories, plans, reports, and other artefacts.

But the deeper problem is not overengineering alone.

**It is that product teams are choosing to optimise low-leverage proxies rather than high-leverage judgment.**

That is why the issue is more serious than it first appears.

Product should be the place where leverage is understood best.  
Product should be the place where problem space is separated from solution space most clearly.  
Product should be the place where expected return, opportunity cost, and sequencing are handled with the most discipline.

So when product teams use AI first to optimise what was never the real bottleneck, they are not simply making a tooling mistake.

They are revealing that they may not understand what product work actually is.

And that is a much bigger problem than a badly written user story.
