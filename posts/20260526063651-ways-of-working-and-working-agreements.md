There is a common problem in teams and organisations that is rarely explained with enough clarity: people often talk about improving the way they work, but they do not always mean the same thing.

Sometimes they are talking about planning, decision-making, priorities, quality standards, or coordination across teams. At other times, they are talking about response times, meeting behaviour, feedback, or how to handle disagreements. These topics are related, but they do not belong to exactly the same level of team life.

This is where two important ideas come in: **ways of working** and **working agreements**.

These expressions are often used as if they meant the same thing. In practice, however, they refer to different layers of how a team operates. Understanding that difference matters. Without it, teams tend to mix structural issues with behavioural ones, and that usually leads to weak diagnosis, vague conversations, and poor improvement efforts.

This article explains what **ways of working** are, what **working agreements** are, and why it is useful to distinguish clearly between them.

The expression **ways of working** refers to the shared framework that defines **how work happens** in a team, a project, or an organisation.

This is an important point: ways of working are not mainly about **what** a team delivers. They are about **how** the team organises, coordinates, executes, reviews, and improves that work over time.

In other words, ways of working describe the operating logic of a team.

They include the principles, practices, standards, rhythms, responsibilities, and mechanisms that make work possible in a consistent and understandable way. They shape how tasks move from idea to execution, how decisions are made, how quality is maintained, and how learning is built into the system.

A team’s ways of working may include questions such as these:

*   How is work planned?

*   How is work prioritised?

*   How are responsibilities distributed?

*   How are decisions made?

*   How is progress tracked?

*   How are dependencies managed?

*   How is quality protected?

*   How does the team review and improve its own system?

Seen from this perspective, ways of working are not a collection of isolated habits. They are the broader structure that allows a team to function with coherence.

Every team has ways of working, even when nobody has written them down.

If they are not explicit, they still exist in practice. They appear in routines, assumptions, informal rules, and repeated behaviours. The problem is that unspoken ways of working often create confusion. People follow different expectations, responsibilities become blurred, and decisions depend too much on personal interpretation.

Making ways of working explicit helps a team create a shared understanding of how work is meant to flow. That does not remove complexity, but it makes complexity easier to manage.

Well-defined ways of working help teams:

*   Reduce ambiguity

*   Align expectations

*   Improve coordination

*   Create more consistent quality

*   Make work more visible

*   Onboard new people more effectively and build a stronger basis for continuous improvement.

Because ways of working describe the operating framework of the team, they usually include structural and systemic elements.

These may include:

*   The overall model of work, such as working in cycles or in continuous flow

*   Planning and review rhythms

*   Prioritisation criteria

*   Roles and responsibilities

*   Rules for escalation and decision-making

*   Quality expectations

*   How work is visualised and tracked

*   How handovers or dependencies are managed and the criteria that define when work is ready to begin and when it can be considered complete.

This last point is especially important. Criteria for readiness and completion are not just casual preferences. They are part of the way the work system protects quality and consistency. For that reason, they belong more naturally to ways of working than to team behaviour alone.

Imagine a product team that says the following:

*   Work is planned every Monday

*   Priorities are reviewed weekly

*   Tasks are visible on a shared board

*   Important changes require review before release

*   Progress is checked at the end of each week and the team reflects regularly on how to improve its system.

These are not random customs. Together, they describe the team’s way of operating. That is what ways of working are: the broader pattern that gives shape to how work happens.

If ways of working define how the work system operates, **working agreements** define how the people inside that system agree to collaborate day by day.

A working agreement is an explicit agreement within a team about how people will communicate, coordinate, support one another, and behave while doing the work.

This is a narrower concept than ways of working, but it is no less important.

Where ways of working describe the broader operating framework, working agreements focus on the practical and relational layer of teamwork. They make expectations visible in places where confusion tends to arise: response times, meetings, feedback, interruptions, escalation, requests for help, review practices, and collaboration norms.

Working agreements answer questions such as:

*   How quickly do we respond to internal messages?

*   How do we signal urgency?

*   What do we do when work becomes blocked?

*   How do we behave in meetings?

*   How do we give feedback?

*   How do we handle disagreement?

*   How do we review each other’s work?

*   How do we coordinate around sensitive technical changes?

In that sense, working agreements do not define the entire work system. They define the agreed behaviours and interaction rules that help people function well inside that system.

Teams often assume that collaboration will take care of itself. It rarely does.

Without explicit agreements, people tend to work from personal habits. One person may expect fast replies, another may value uninterrupted focus. One person may see direct feedback as helpful, while another may experience it as abrupt. One person may escalate quickly, while another may wait too long.

None of these tensions necessarily come from bad intent. More often, they come from unspoken expectations.

Working agreements reduce that problem by turning assumptions into shared commitments.

They help teams:

*   Clarify expectations

*   Reduce avoidable friction

*   Improve communication

*   Make collaboration more predictable

*   Strengthen accountability and create healthier working relationships.

They are especially valuable in teams that are cross-functional, distributed, fast-growing, or under pressure, because in those environments the cost of ambiguity rises quickly.

A common mistake is to think that working agreements only concern soft topics such as tone, manners, or meeting etiquette.

They can include those things, but they can also include technical agreements, as long as the purpose is to align how people work together.

For example, a team may agree:

*   That certain changes require an additional review

*   That specific checks must be completed before integration

*   That technical decisions affecting others must be documented or that urgent incidents must be communicated through a specific channel.

These are technical in content, but collaborative in function. They regulate how people coordinate around technical work.

That is why working agreements should not be reduced to rules of politeness. They are better understood as explicit agreements about day-to-day collaboration, including relational, operational, and technical aspects where relevant.

Imagine that the same product team also agrees on the following:

*   Internal messages should be answered within one working day

*   Blocked work should be raised on the same day

*   Meetings begin on time

*   Feedback should focus on the work, not on the person

*   Major technical changes require an additional review and decisions made in meetings must be written down afterwards.

These agreements do not define the whole work system. They define how people act within it. That is the role of working agreements.

At this point, the connection between the two concepts becomes clearer.

Ways of working and working agreements are related, but they are not interchangeable.

The simplest way to understand the relationship is this:

*   **Ways of working** define how work functions as a system.

*   **Working agreements** define how people collaborate within that system.

This means that working agreements often sit inside the wider frame created by ways of working. They help make the operating model workable in everyday practice.

If ways of working provide the architecture of work, working agreements provide some of the social and practical rules that allow people to move inside that architecture effectively.

This distinction matters because not every problem belongs to the same layer.

If a team struggles with unclear priorities, weak flow, or inconsistent quality standards, the issue may belong to ways of working.

If a team struggles with poor feedback, delayed responses, unclear meeting behaviour, or lack of coordination, the issue may belong to working agreements.

When teams mix these layers, they often solve the wrong problem.

The difference between the two concepts becomes easier to see when they are compared directly.

Ways of working have a broader scope. They describe the overall framework through which work is organised and delivered.

Working agreements have a narrower scope. They describe specific agreements about how people interact and collaborate within that framework.

Ways of working focus on the operation of the work system: planning, flow, priorities, decision-making, quality, visibility, and improvement.

Working agreements focus on the day-to-day dynamics of collaboration: communication, coordination, meetings, feedback, escalation, and shared expectations.

Ways of working are structural and systemic.

Working agreements are practical and relational, although they can also include technical agreements when those agreements regulate collaboration.

Ways of working create consistency in how work is organised and executed.

Working agreements create clarity in how people work together while that work is being organised and executed.

Examples of ways of working include:

*   Plnning work in regular cycles

*   Using a shared board to manage flow

*   Prioritising by impact and urgency

*   Defining clear quality standards

*   Establishing criteria for when work can start and establishing criteria for when work can be considered complete.

Examples of working agreements include:

*   Answering internal messages within an agreed time

*   Raising blockers on the same day

*   Beginning meetings on time

*   Giving feedback on the work rather than on the individual

*   Documenting decisions after discussions and requesting additional review for sensitive changes.

When it is not obvious whether something belongs to ways of working or working agreements, one simple test can help.

Ask this question:

**Is this mainly about how the work system functions, or is it mainly about how people coordinate within that system?**

If it is mainly about how work flows, how decisions are structured, how quality is protected, or how the operating model is designed, it is probably part of ways of working.

If it is mainly about how people communicate, collaborate, respond, review, help, or behave in practice, it is probably a working agreement.

This test is not perfect in every edge case, but it is strong enough to create much more clarity than treating both ideas as the same thing.

It can be tempting to dismiss this distinction as a matter of terminology. It is not.

The words teams use shape the way they diagnose problems and design improvements.

If everything is treated as one blurred category, conversations become vague. Teams say they need to “improve how we work,” but they do not know whether they need to redesign their operating model or simply clarify their agreements with one another.

A team that does not distinguish between these levels may try to fix a structural problem with a behavioural rule, or try to solve a collaboration issue by changing a process that was not the real cause.

That is why the distinction matters. It supports better thinking, better language, and better intervention.

It helps teams ask sharper questions:

*   Is this a system issue or a collaboration issue?

*   Do we need to redesign how work flows, or do we need to clarify expectations between people?

*   Are we changing the operating model, or are we updating our agreements about daily interaction?

Those are not minor differences. They are the basis of more rigorous team improvement.

Every team works in two ways at the same time.

It works through a system: priorities, rhythms, roles, standards, decisions, and flow.

It also works through relationships: communication, trust, coordination, feedback, and shared habits.

Ways of working help define the first. Working agreements help shape the second.

Both matter. Neither replaces the other. And the better a team understands the difference, the more effectively it can improve not only what it does, but how it does it together.

Ways of working are the shared framework that defines how work is organised, executed, and improved. Working agreements are the explicit agreements that define how people communicate, coordinate, and collaborate within that framework.

Understanding the difference between them is not an academic exercise. It is a practical step towards clearer teamwork, better decisions, and more intentional organisations.
