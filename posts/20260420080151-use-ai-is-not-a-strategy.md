Title: Use AI is not a strategy

Final URL: https://emmanuelvalverderamos.substack.com/p/use-ai-is-not-a-strategy

Markdown Content:
**Every time I say AI on this article I’m talking about LLM and agents, and also MCP, because all of this elements ARE NOT AI. But everybody call this AI**

If you tell a team to drive around Barcelona, they can spend the whole day moving and still fail by nightfall.

They can burn fuel.  
They can take longer routes.  
They can feel busy.  
They can generate a beautiful dashboard full of activity.

But unless someone can answer where they were supposed to go, why that destination mattered, what counted as success, and whether the route improved anything important, none of that movement means much.

That is what a lot of so-called AI strategy looks like right now.

Not strategy.  
Not judgment.  
Not a coherent theory of improvement.

Just motion with better branding.

Most companies do not have an AI strategy. They have an AI instruction.

They tell people to use LLMs, copilots, or agents without defining what problem should improve, what trade-offs matter, what risks are acceptable, what the baseline is, or how progress will be judged. That is why so much of this collapses into token counts, vague pressure, and adoption theatre. A real strategy would start with diagnosis, make choices, constrain action, and judge results economically: value, cost, risk, flexibility, and the finite capacity of the team.

When executives say AI today, they usually mean something much narrower than the phrase suggests. They mean LLMs, copilots, agents, retrieval systems, prompt workflows, and automation layers.

That distinction matters because it removes the mystique. The moment you stop speaking as if this were some general intelligence descending from the clouds and start naming the actual tools in front of you, the organisational failure becomes easier to see.

Use it.  
Adopt it.  
Experiment with it.  
Integrate it into your workflow.  
Be more productive.

All of that may be well intentioned. None of it is strategy.

A command is not a strategy because it came from leadership.  
A mandate is not a strategy because budget was assigned to it.  
Adoption is not a strategy because it appears on a slide.

This is the first failure. Many companies confuse ambition with strategy. They think wanting something loudly is evidence of having thought clearly. It is not.

Strategy is not aspiration with executive sponsorship. Strategy is a disciplined response to a real challenge. It begins by naming the obstacle honestly. Then it chooses an approach. Then it aligns actions around that approach.

Without that structure, there is no strategy. There is only pressure.

That is why so much AI language inside companies feels hollow. It skips diagnosis. It avoids hard choices. It leaves conflicts unresolved. It wants speed, safety, innovation, cost reduction, better quality, more output, less risk, and instant cultural transformation, all at once.

That is not strategic sophistication.

That is organisational cowardice wearing modern vocabulary.

This is where the language usually falls apart.

A strategy is not a roadmap.  
A strategy is not a timeline.  
A strategy is not a calendar of initiatives.  
A strategy is not “we will roll out AI to every team in Q3.”  
A strategy is not “we have selected vendors and funded a programme.”

Those things may sit around strategy. They may help operationalise it. They may communicate sequence and commitment. But they are not the thing itself.

A roadmap tells you what is expected to happen, in what order, over what period, and often under what dependencies. That can be useful. Organisations do need coordination.

But a roadmap does not answer the harder question: why this path, in this context, against this challenge, with these trade-offs, rather than another one?

That question belongs to strategy.

If a company cannot explain the problem it is trying to solve, the advantage it hopes to create, the constraints it must respect, and the logic behind its chosen approach, then a roadmap is just an orderly schedule for wandering.

Tactics sit lower still. They are the practical moves that make a strategy real. They are the bounded experiments, workflow changes, guardrails, review rules, communication routines, decision rights, rollout patterns, and local manoeuvres that fit the actual terrain.

That distinction matters because many organisations jump straight from slogan to tooling. They do not diagnose the challenge, choose an approach, and then define tactics. They buy the tool first and call the purchase progress.

But the practical layer is exactly where the truth shows up.

If the real problem is that support teams waste hours reconstructing context from scattered tickets, the tactic might be retrieval plus draft assistance with human review.

If the real problem is that engineers lose time navigating fragmented internal knowledge, the tactic might be tightly scoped internal documentation support, not broad autonomous agents.

If the real problem is that product decisions are slow because information is scattered across too many systems, the tactic might be synthesis support in discovery, not a company-wide order to “use AI more.”

That is what tactics are for. They are not grand statements. They are practical moves in service of a strategic choice.

The Barcelona metaphor is not decoration. It is the whole problem.

Imagine I tell you to drive but give you no destination. At the end of the day, what exactly are we evaluating?

Distance travelled?  
Fuel consumed?  
Time on the road?  
Number of turns taken?  
How often you touched the wheel?

All of those are real measurements. None of them answer the question that matters.

Were you moving toward anything worth reaching?

This is what token dashboards often do inside companies. They elevate the most visible unit of activity into a false unit of value. Prompts, sessions, licences, copilots enabled, automations created, tokens consumed. These numbers exist, so they become seductive. But existing is not the same thing as mattering.

A token is a unit of consumption.  
It is not a unit of value.

It does not tell you whether decisions improved, whether rework fell, whether lead time changed in a meaningful way, whether support quality improved, whether customer outcomes moved, or whether scarce human attention was redirected toward something more valuable.

If all you know is that usage increased, then all you really know is that usage increased.

That is not nothing.

But it is nowhere near enough.

The deeper failure is economic. Companies measure activity because value is harder to define. Yet that difficulty is not an excuse. It is the work. In product and software, every meaningful decision is already economic whether people name it that way or not. You are always trading among value, cost, risk, and flexibility. The only question is whether you are doing it consciously.

This is where the whole conversation becomes less vague.

Stop treating AI as a technology conversation and start treating it as an investment conversation.

In software, the goal is not to produce activity. The goal is to protect and improve an investment.

That means at least five things.

First, value. What is becoming more useful, more effective, more differentiated, or more meaningful to the customer or the business?

Second, cost. Not only the initial implementation cost, but the continuing cost of operating, correcting, integrating, training, reviewing, maintaining, and explaining whatever you introduce.

Third, risk. What new failure modes are you adding? Hallucinated answers, brittle automations, lower judgment quality, compliance breaches, hidden dependencies, or workflows no one really understands.

Fourth, flexibility. How easily can you change course if the assumptions prove wrong? How much budget, architecture, process, and organisational attention have you tied up in a direction that may not pay off?

Fifth, capacity. Every system you add consumes some permanent share of a team’s future ability to do useful work. That includes not just code, but prompts, scaffolding, evaluation harnesses, guardrails, integrations, and the human routines required to keep the whole thing trustworthy.

That last point is the one most AI enthusiasm tries hardest not to see.

Every new capability comes with maintenance gravity.

If you add an agent, you do not just add a feature. You add operational care, monitoring, trust calibration, edge cases, ownership questions, user expectations, and future change cost. Unnecessary additions do not merely waste effort once. They keep consuming capacity over time.

Badly chosen AI initiatives can become one more layer of recurring drag on a team that was already overloaded.

That is why “more AI” is such a weak objective.

More of what, exactly?  
More value?  
More leverage on the real constraint?  
More useful decisions?  
Or just more moving parts for the team to carry forever?

A real strategy would begin with diagnosis.

Where is work slow?  
Where is it fragile?  
Where is context fragmented?  
Where are people wasting time reconstructing information that should already be usable?  
Where does speed matter?  
Where does judgment matter more than speed?  
Where is the cost of error low enough to experiment safely?  
Where is the cost of error high enough that automation is irresponsible?

Then it would choose a guiding policy.

Not “use AI everywhere.”  
Not “every team should explore use cases.”  
Not “increase adoption.”

Something more like this: reduce the time spent reconstructing internal context in low-risk knowledge workflows while preserving human review and keeping model usage out of customer-facing decision points until quality thresholds are proven.

That is a policy. It draws a line. It constrains. It prioritises. It implies what matters and what does not.

Then come the coherent actions.

Pick the workflows in scope.  
Define the baseline.  
Choose the evaluation criteria.  
Decide what stays human-reviewed.  
Design training and usage patterns.  
Set guardrails.  
Run bounded experiments.  
Retire dead ends quickly.  
Scale only what proves useful.  
Stop doing the things the new system genuinely makes unnecessary.

That is what strategy looks like once it leaves the slide deck and enters the real world.

Notice what disappears the moment you think this way: empty adoption theatre.

The conversation stops being “how do we get people to use more AI?” and becomes “what problem are we solving, by what mechanism, at what cost, with what risks, and what evidence will tell us it is working?”

That is a much less fashionable question.

It is also the only useful one.

Because real strategy is restrictive.

It forces a company to choose.  
It makes trade-offs visible.  
It makes failure legible.  
It turns vague enthusiasm into something that can actually be judged.

That is uncomfortable.

It is easier to announce a company-wide AI push than to admit that only a few workflows have a credible case today. It is easier to count usage than to define value. It is easier to buy tools than to redesign work. It is easier to pressure teams than to sharpen management thinking.

And there is one more uncomfortable truth.

Many organisations do not merely lack an AI strategy. They lack the organisational clarity required to form one. The moment they try, all the older problems surface: unclear ownership, weak workflows, poor knowledge management, undefined quality standards, conflicting incentives, and no real agreement on what “better” means.

AI did not create that confusion.

It exposed it.

“Use AI” is not a strategy.

It is an instruction.  
Sometimes an anxious one.  
Sometimes a fashionable one.  
Sometimes a politically convenient one.

But still just an instruction.

A strategy would be able to explain why this organisation, in this context, with these constraints, should apply LLMs or agents in these specific places, for these reasons, with these guardrails, through these tactics, in pursuit of these outcomes.

A roadmap could then sequence that work.  
Tactics could then make it executable.  
Measurement could then test whether the investment is actually being protected.

But without that order, companies end up with the worst of both worlds: lots of activity, very little clarity, and a growing pile of systems whose long-term cost nobody priced in honestly.

There is also a more personal reading of all this.

A great deal is being said about replacing software engineers because producing code is becoming easier. Part of the point of this piece is to take that logic seriously enough to expose how shallow it is. If the bar is now just producing plausible-sounding outputs quickly, then many C-level leaders and middle managers are standing on weaker ground than the engineers they talk about replacing.

Large language models hallucinate, invent, and say things with confidence when they are wrong. But so do plenty of C-level leaders and middle managers discussing AI. The difference is that the model has at least been trained on a vast body of information. Many of these people have no such excuse. They still confuse a strategy with an order, a roadmap with strategy, activity with progress, and dashboards with judgment.

Imagine a war in which the strategy is simply: defeat the enemy.

No diagnosis of the situation.  
No understanding of terrain.  
No assessment of your own capabilities.  
No idea where advantage comes from.  
No logistics.  
No contingencies.  
No coherent plan of action.

It would be absurd.

Yet that is remarkably close to how many companies talk about AI. Use it. Adopt it. Push it into the organisation. Track consumption. Report activity. Declare momentum.

That is not strategic thinking.

That is managerial simplification at its worst.

So no, the real lesson here is not that software engineers are uniquely replaceable because some parts of code production are becoming easier. The deeper lesson is that anyone whose role has been reduced to vague instruction, shallow abstraction, plausible-sounding outputs, and confidence without diagnosis is standing on much weaker ground than they think.

That is the real exposure.

Not that machines can generate code.

That many leaders still cannot tell the difference between a strategy and an order.

And that is why so many companies are now doing the corporate equivalent of driving around Barcelona all day, burning fuel, filling dashboards, and calling motion direction.
