Title: Product strategy is not a roadmap, and a roadmap is not a timeline

Markdown Content:
The most common product artifact is also one of the most conceptually confused. Teams call a roadmap a strategy, then use a timeline as if it were a roadmap, and end up treating a schedule as if it were strategic thinking. That sounds harmless. It is not. Strategy, roadmap, tactics, and timeline answer different questions. Strategy defines the challenge and the logic of response. Tactics are the concrete moves made within that logic. A roadmap communicates the staged path of progress. A timeline schedules specific actions and dependencies. Once those distinctions collapse, product work becomes theatre of certainty rather than disciplined movement under uncertainty.

*   Product strategy is not a dated plan. It is a coherent response to a defining challenge.

*   A roadmap is not the strategy itself. It is a communication artifact that expresses intended movement, sequence, and major decision points.

*   Tactics are not strategy either. They are the specific moves that make sense because a strategy gives them coherence.

*   A timeline or Gantt chart is not useless. It is just narrower than people pretend. It coordinates commitments. It does not justify them.

*   Richard Rumelt’s strategy framing is especially useful here: diagnosis, guiding policy, and coherent action. That is much stricter than goals, themes, or wish lists.

*   Marty Cagan’s critique matters because it exposes the product-specific failure mode: feature roadmaps are often treated as commitments, while many product ideas fail on value, usability, feasibility, or viability, and even successful ideas usually require iteration before they deliver business results.

*   The practical answer is not to stop planning. It is to stop asking one artifact to do five different jobs.

*   A schedule coordinates movement. Strategy justifies it.

Most product organisations do not lack plans. They lack distinctions.

Ask for the strategy and someone shows you a roadmap. Ask for the roadmap and someone shows you a quarterly plan. Ask for the plan and someone shows you a timeline with dates, dependencies, and coloured bars. Somewhere in that slide deck, strategy quietly disappears.

This is not a vocabulary problem. It is an operating problem.

What looks like alignment is often compression. Four different questions get squeezed into one artifact:

*   What challenge actually matters?

*   What logic are we choosing to deal with it?

*   What moves will we make inside that logic?

*   What work is expected by when?

Those are not the same question. They require different kinds of judgment, different levels of confidence, and different kinds of evidence.

A strategy is not a calendar.  
A roadmap is not a contract.  
A timeline is not a theory of progress.

That is the first reframing.

Because once a company starts treating a dated list of outputs as strategy, it stops asking the harder questions. Why this problem? Why this sequence? Why this market? Why this route? What must be true for this to work? What are we deliberately not doing?

The artifact becomes comforting precisely because it is visually orderly. Bars, boxes, milestones, and dates create the impression of control.

But control is not the same as strategy.

The confusion persists because all these artifacts point at the future.

That makes them easy to blur together.

Finance needs planning assumptions. Sales wants expectations. Marketing wants launch windows. Leadership wants coordination. Teams want clarity. None of that is irrational. In many organisations, some form of roadmap and some form of schedule are genuinely necessary.

That is exactly why the confusion is dangerous. It is built on legitimate pressure.

The usual defense sounds sensible: the business needs a roadmap because the business needs to plan.

True. But that sentence hides a substitution.

Planning is necessary, so the roadmap becomes a plan.  
The plan is important, so it becomes a commitment.  
The commitment is visible, so it starts to masquerade as strategy.

That progression is understandable. It is also wrong.

The hidden assumption is that future-facing coordination artifacts can do the work of strategic reasoning. They cannot. They answer different questions.

A timeline answers: when are these actions expected to happen?

A roadmap answers: how are we communicating intended movement, sequence, and major decision points?

A strategy answers: what is the defining challenge, what logic are we using to deal with it, and what coherent choices follow from that?

A tactic answers: what concrete move are we making inside that logic?

When those layers collapse, the proxy becomes the goal.

A dictionary-style definition of strategy as a plan of action designed to achieve a long-term or overall aim is not wrong. It is just too broad for product work.

Why? Because under that definition, almost any serious plan can be mistaken for strategy. A roadmap can. A delivery plan can. A quarterly initiative list can. A Gantt chart can.

That is not precise enough.

Because it puts the challenge back at the centre. As he writes, “A good strategy has an essential logical structure that I call the kernel.” He then defines that kernel through diagnosis, guiding policy, and coherent action.

That is a much stricter idea than “our priorities for the year.”

It forces contact with reality.  
It forces choice.  
It forces trade-offs.

This is the second aha moment.

What many teams call strategy is actually a mixture of goals, themes, and requested work. But goals do not tell you how the problem is understood. Themes do not tell you what trade-offs were chosen. Requested work does not tell you why this path makes more sense than another plausible path.

Strategy starts with a diagnosis. Not with a wish list.

Without that, a company may still have activity. It does not yet have strategy.

It is important not to overcorrect.

The article is not making the childish claim that roadmaps are useless. It is making the stricter claim that a roadmap has a narrower job than many companies assign to it.

A roadmap is a communication artifact. At its best, it communicates intended movement across time, stages, focus areas, and major decision points. It can express the sequence of bets on the path toward a product vision. It can make strategic intent legible.

But it is not the strategy itself.

It is not the diagnosis.  
It is not the core logic of response.  
It is not the governing set of choices.

It is the communication of how that intent is expected to unfold.

That distinction matters because the undisciplined version of a roadmap usually becomes something else entirely: a prioritised list of features and projects with dates attached. The typical product roadmap are usually about output, include requested features, projects, and initiatives, and normally include due dates or time frames.

So the problem is not that the desire for a roadmap is irrational.

The problem is what happens next.

Once a feature-and-date roadmap enters the organisation, it stops acting like a navigational aid and starts acting like a promise register. The roadmap may still be labelled “directional,” but the rest of the company often interprets it as commitment

> “people across the company will interpret the items as a commitment.”
> 
> 
> - Marty Cagan

That is the danger.

A roadmap can express strategy.  
But once it pretends uncertainty has already been resolved, it stops clarifying and starts distorting.

This is where precision matters.

The strongest parts of the argument do not all rest on the same kind of evidence.

His argument is sharp. Typical product roadmaps often lead to poor business results because at least half of product ideas will not work, and they can fail for several reasons: lack of value, lack of usability, lack of feasibility, or lack of business viability. Even when ideas survive those tests, they usually need several iterations before they deliver the expected business value. On top of that, once ideas are placed on a document called “roadmap,” people across the company tend to interpret them as commitments.

That does not prove a universal law. It does expose a mechanism that many product organisations will recognise immediately.

And that mechanism is plausible.

If many ideas will fail, and successful ones usually need iteration, then a dated feature list is not merely optimistic. It is brittle.

This is the third aha moment.

The classic roadmap is not mainly dangerous because dates slip.  
It is dangerous because it creates social certainty before the organisation has earned epistemic certainty.

The issue is not delay.  
It is premature commitment.

And once the map becomes the contract, learning starts to look like failure.

A simple travel example makes the distinction clearer.

Suppose the goal is to get from Madrid to Rome.

A timeline says:

Leave Madrid at 08:00.  
Arrive at the first stop at 12:30.  
Board the next connection at 13:10.  
Arrive in Rome the next morning.

That may be a schedule. It may even be a useful one. But it is not a strategy, and it is not a roadmap in any meaningful sense.

Why not?

Because it tells us when things are supposed to happen, but almost nothing about the actual challenge. Are we optimising for speed, cost, reliability, comfort, safety, learning, or flexibility? Are we trying to avoid fragile transfers? Are we travelling through stable hubs because disruption risk is high? Are we preserving fallback options because a missed connection would collapse the whole trip?

Without that, we do not have strategy.  
We have sequencing.

A strategy for getting from Madrid to Rome might be this:

Our real challenge is not movement itself but arriving reliably and at acceptable cost under uncertain travel conditions. We will therefore optimise for reliability and recoverability over minimum travel time. We will avoid brittle, transfer-heavy options, favour routes that pass through major hubs with stronger recovery paths, and keep enough flexibility at key points so that one disruption does not ruin the journey.

Now we have something strategic.

We have a diagnosis: the challenge is not merely distance but fragile movement under uncertainty.

We have a guiding policy: prefer reliable, recoverable routes over theoretically faster but more failure-prone ones.

We have coherent implications: do not over-optimise for speed, preserve recovery options, and choose paths whose failures do not cascade.

Now tactics become clearer too.

Tactics are the concrete moves taken within that strategic logic. In this example, tactics might include choosing a major hub instead of a smaller interchange, buying cancellable tickets on the riskier legs, keeping luggage light to preserve mobility, and delaying the final booking until the first leg has gone well.

That is a helpful distinction.

Tactics are moves.  
Strategy is the logic that makes the moves make sense together.

A roadmap would then communicate how the strategy unfolds across stages:

*   Leave Madrid on the highest-confidence first leg

*   Reach a major hub with multiple continuation options

*   Reassess conditions at the first decision point

*   Preserve an overnight flexibility buffer if disruption risk rises

*   Commit to the final route into Rome once conditions are clearer

That is closer to a roadmap.

It communicates intended movement, sequence, and decision logic, but it still does not pretend every operational detail is already fixed.

Only then does a timeline make sense.

Once bookings are made and the relevant uncertainty has been reduced, the timeline can specify the actual trains, departure times, connection windows, and arrival expectations.

That is the distinction.

Strategy explains the logic of the journey.  
Tactics are the concrete moves inside that logic.  
The roadmap communicates the staged path.  
The timeline schedules the specific actions.

Confuse them, and you get a beautifully organised trip built on shallow thinking.

The same mistake becomes even clearer in football.

A football team has a strategy. The left-back does not have one strategy, the striker another, the goalkeeper a third, and the midfield yet another, all operating independently as if the match were a collection of personal plans.

If that happened, it would not be strategy.  
It would be chaos.

A real football strategy is shared. It aligns roles, space, timing, trade-offs, and intent. One team may press high, compress the pitch, and try to recover the ball within seconds of losing it. Another may defend deep, concede territory, and attack in transition. Different strategies are possible. But in every case, the strategy is not the sum of isolated preferences. It is the coordinating logic that makes separate actions additive rather than contradictory.

This is exactly what many product organisations miss.

A strategy is not a pile of local plans.  
It is not each team shipping its own roadmap.  
It is not each department pursuing its own priorities with a thin layer of executive language on top.

It is the coherence that makes distributed action make sense together.

The tactics in football are the specific moves that serve that strategy: the press trigger on a backward pass, the midfield shape, the defensive line height, the choice to overload one flank, the decision to force play wide. Those tactics can change. The strategy remains the larger logic.

That is why the analogy is useful.

A team does not win because eleven players each had a plan.  
A team wins because eleven players were playing the same game.

This is the point where some people overreact.

They hear “a Gantt chart is not strategy” and translate it into “planning is bad.”

No.

The problem is not planning.  
The problem is category inflation.

A Gantt chart can be useful when the work is understood well enough, dependencies matter, and commitments are real enough to coordinate against. Construction projects use them. Infrastructure programmes use them. Certain regulated delivery efforts need them. Even product organisations may need schedule-level planning for launches with legal, partner, commercial, or operational commitments.

But none of that turns a Gantt chart into strategy.

A Gantt chart coordinates known work over time. Its strength is explicit coordination. Its weakness is that the visual confidence of the chart can exceed the actual confidence of the underlying knowledge.

In product, much of the uncertainty is not just execution uncertainty. It is problem uncertainty and solution uncertainty. We often do not yet know whether the chosen idea will solve the problem well enough, whether customers will value it enough, whether they will understand it enough, whether it can be built at an acceptable cost, or whether it is viable for the business. A schedule can sequence the work. It cannot answer those questions for free.

What teams often treat as foresight is sometimes just formatting.

Bars on a timeline do not make the premise more true.

Once the distinctions are clean, the alternative becomes easier to see.

You do not need one artifact that pretends to solve everything. You need several artifacts and practices, each doing an honest job.

The product vision describes the future you are trying to create. It is further out, more directional than operational, and meant to align and inspire.

The product strategy defines the challenge, the chosen logic of response, the major trade-offs, and the meaningful bets on the path toward the vision.

These translate strategy into measurable outcomes. Not feature quotas. Business results.

These are the specific moves teams make within the strategic logic. They may change more often than the strategy itself.

The roadmap communicates staged progress, focus areas, major decision points, and, where needed, carefully bounded commitments.

Only after uncertainty has been sufficiently reduced should teams attach specific dates and dependencies to specific work.

This model is less theatrical and more demanding. It removes the fantasy that one slide can satisfy strategy, planning, discovery, stakeholder reassurance, and scheduling at the same time.

That fantasy is expensive.

There is another distinction underneath all this.

Typical product roadmaps are output-shaped.  
Good product strategy is outcome-shaped.

That does not mean outputs are irrelevant. Products still have to be built and shipped. But outputs answer a smaller question: what did we deliver? Outcomes answer the harder one: what changed, for whom, and in what way that matters?

Typical product roadmaps are about output, while good teams are asked to deliver business results. He also frames strong product practice around business objectives and the need to give discovery time before making commitments.

This is why a roadmap full of features is often a poor strategic artifact.

Features are possible means.  
They are not proof that the right problem has been chosen.  
They are not proof that the selected solution will work.

Feature-first planning feels concrete. But it often smuggles untested assumptions past scrutiny.

The artifact looks disciplined.  
The reasoning underneath may not be.

That is why accountability should move upward, not downward.

From shipping the requested feature to solving the relevant problem.

If this confusion is so costly, why does it survive?

Because the mixed artifact is politically useful.

A single dated roadmap does several comforting things at once:

*   It makes leadership feel informed

*   It makes stakeholders feel heard

*   It helps adjacent functions plan around assumptions

*   It makes progress look visible

*   It turns uncertainty into something legible

That is seductive.

But the fact that an artifact reduces anxiety does not mean it improves decisions.

In many companies, the roadmap is defended less because it is strategically sound and more because it is socially convenient. It creates a common performance surface. Everyone can point at it. Everyone can ask for updates. Everyone can attach expectations.

That is why simply saying “roadmaps are bad” is too crude.

The roadmap is solving real reassurance and coordination problems, even while it creates strategic distortion.

So the mature move is not roadmap abolition.

It is roadmap demotion.

Give it a smaller, clearer job.

Most organisations cannot jump overnight from feature-commitment roadmaps to a clean outcome-oriented model. Nor should they pretend they can.

A better transition is incremental.

If your current “strategy” slide is actually a mixture of aspiration, targets, and themes, split it.

Not always. But more often than most companies think.

Make clear which statements describe the challenge and logic of response, and which describe specific moves.

Some dates are real commitments. Fine. Mark them as such. Keep them few and in case that something else comes up as a commitment, then ask what would it replace if you do not have more capability.

Marty Cagan on the book INSPIRE mentionts that the alternative is not “never commit.” It is “stop committing too early.” High-integrity commitments come after enough discovery to know whether the solution is valuable, usable, feasible, and viable.

Reduce feature granularity. Increase focus on problem spaces, decision points, intended outcomes, and major bets.

Let schedule-level artifacts do schedule-level work.

This does not remove tension.  
It surfaces it honestly.

And that is progress.

There is a serious counterargument here.

Large organisations have budgets, annual planning cycles, contracts, launches, staffing decisions, and partner dependencies. They need dates. They need plans. They need to know what is happening when.

That is true.

The disciplined position is not that dates are naive. It is that dates must be attached at the right level, with the right confidence, for the right reason.

Where work is known enough and commitments are real, schedule it.

Where uncertainty is still high, do not pretend it is low merely because another function wants reassurance.

The mature organisation runs both modes at once. It preserves strategic and discovery flexibility where uncertainty is real, and it makes high-integrity commitments where the business genuinely needs them and the knowledge is strong enough to support them.

The mistake is not commitment.  
The mistake is counterfeit commitment.

There is a simple test.

**If I remove all dates from your artifact, do I still understand the defining challenge**, **the trade-offs, the logic of response, and why this path makes sense?**

If yes, you may have a strategy.

If no, you probably had a schedule decorated with ambition.

A second test is just as useful.

**If I remove all feature names from your roadmap, do I still understand intended movement, sequence, major bets, and decision points?**

If yes, you may have a roadmap.

If no, you probably had a backlog with executive branding.

And there is a third test.

**If the tactics change, does the strategy remain intelligible?**  
It should.

**If the timeline slips, does the strategy remain intelligible?**  
It should.

Because strategy is not the date and not the move.

It is the logic that makes the movement worth making at all.

The central mistake in product is not lack of planning. It is conceptual collapse.

Strategy, roadmap, tactics, and timeline are not four labels for the same artifact.

Strategy defines the challenge and the logic of response.  
Tactics are the moves made within that logic.  
A roadmap communicates the staged path.  
A timeline schedules the specific actions.

Collapse them and you get theatre: visible movement, hidden confusion, and false certainty masquerading as leadership.
