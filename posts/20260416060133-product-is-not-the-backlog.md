# Product is not the backlog

### Product is not there to process requests. Engineering is not there to receive them. The real work starts when both sides help decide what deserves to exist, why it matters,...

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!FaRL!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F66f61688-aac8-4df0-b366-8afdc228d44a_1024x559.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!FaRL!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F66f61688-aac8-4df0-b366-8afdc228d44a_1024x559.png)

A lot of companies say they have product teams when what they really have is a routing layer between stakeholders and engineers. The language sounds modern, but the operating model is old. Requests come in, priorities get negotiated, requirements get written, and engineering is expected to deliver. Product is not backlog administration. It is judgment under uncertainty about what to do, why to do it, what it is for, how it fits the strategy, what it adds to the product and the company, and what should happen first. Engineering is not downstream delivery. It is part of shaping whether a solution is feasible, sound, sustainable, and worth the technical cost it will impose later. The strongest support available here is practitioner-led, first-hand, and conceptual rather than peer-reviewed causal research, so the argument should be read at that level. But even within that limit, the pattern is clear enough: weak teams separate thinking from building, then call the gap “alignment.” Stronger teams close that gap.

## TL;DR

- Product should answer a specific class of questions: what are we doing, why are we doing it, what is it for, how does it fit the product strategy, what does it add to the product and the company, and what should happen first.
- Product should own the **why**, the **what**, and the **strategic how**. Engineering should own the **technical how**.
- Strong product teams are not built on hierarchy and handoff. They are cross-functional teams where product, design, and engineering work out solutions together.
- Discovery is not a product-only phase. It is collaborative work meant to test value, usability, feasibility, and viability before production software is written.
- The conventional sequence of roadmap, requirements, design, build, and late engineering involvement is a weak model for modern product work.
- Sustainable collaboration depends on small steps, fast feedback, restraint, and decisions not only about what to add, but also what not to build and what to remove.
- Product and engineering are better understood as different lenses on the same investment, balancing value, risk, cost, debt, and timing.

## The mistake begins before anyone writes a ticket

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!TBe4!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa390439c-25e4-4b7d-b091-55d551aecc38_1024x559.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!TBe4!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa390439c-25e4-4b7d-b091-55d551aecc38_1024x559.png)

Most companies do not misunderstand engineering first.

They misunderstand product.

They assume product is there to gather requests, clean them up, order them, and pass them on. It looks efficient. It feels organised. It creates just enough structure for everyone to pretend the hard part is over.

It is not.

The hard part is not moving work through the system. The hard part is deciding what work deserves to enter the system at all.

That is why the usual operating model is so deceptive. It turns uncertainty into process and then mistakes the process for clarity. A business case becomes a roadmap. A roadmap becomes requirements. Requirements become design input. Engineering arrives late and is asked for estimates on decisions it did not help shape (and if you ask me they would not estimate in the first place).

That is the first important distinction.

A coordination role keeps work moving.
A product role improves judgment about what is worth moving.

Those are not the same thing.

## Product should answer a specific class of questions

From my point of view, product should answer a specific class of questions.

What are we doing?
Why are we doing it?
What is it for?
How does this fit our product strategy?
What does this add to the product in the long term?
What does this add to the company?
How does this affect company strategy?
What should happen first, given the outcomes we want, the return we expect, and the direction we claim to be following?

That matters because these are not paperwork questions.

They are judgment questions.

They ask product to distinguish noise from signal.
They ask product to distinguish pressure from priority.
They ask product to distinguish movement from strategy.

That is the real job.

## The most useful boundary is inside the word “how”

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!PKPb!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F6c0d0e6a-751d-407d-898e-b11227c5d221_1024x558.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!PKPb!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F6c0d0e6a-751d-407d-898e-b11227c5d221_1024x558.png)

The usual formula says product owns the why and the what, while engineering owns the how.

That is close, but still sloppy.

Because product does have to answer part of the how.

Not the technical how.
The strategic how.

How does this fit the product direction?
How does it create value?
How does it change the product over time?
How does it connect to business priorities?
How should it be sequenced relative to other opportunities?

Those questions belong with product.

The technical how is different.

How do we build this safely?
How do we reduce technical risk?
How do we preserve adaptability?
How do we avoid spending future capacity too early?
How do we sequence the work without turning today’s shortcut into tomorrow’s drag?

Those questions belong with engineering.

This distinction is the cleanest one in the whole article because it explains a lot of otherwise stale conflict.

When product tries to own the technical how, it overreaches.
When engineering refuses to engage with the strategic how, it retreats.
Then both sides start defending the wrong border and call the result collaboration.

It is not collaboration.
It is adjacent decision-making.

## Engineering is not downstream

This is where the common model really starts to collapse.

Discovery and delivery are not separate departments of reality. They are parallel activities inside the same cross-functional team. Discovery is described as intense collaboration between product, design, and engineering, and its purpose is to answer four questions before production software is written: will users buy or use this, can they figure out how to use it, can engineers build it, and can stakeholders support it.

That framing does more than encourage earlier engineering involvement.

It destroys the handoff model.

If feasibility is one of the core questions, engineering cannot be a downstream recipient.
If engineers are expected to try prototypes daily during discovery, they are not there just to estimate after decisions are made and if you ask me, they are not there to estimate nothing.

What many companies call alignment is often just sequencing.
Product decides. Engineering aligns.

That sounds orderly.
It is usually expensive.

Because the decisions that look clean on a slide are often the ones that have not yet touched reality.

## Product should not become project management with better vocabulary

Once the role is seen properly, its limits become easier to name.

Product is not be the boss of engineering.

Product should not become a requirements funnel.

Product should not treat roadmaps as proof of understanding. A roadmap can be a communication device. It is not, by itself, evidence that the team has learned enough to deserve confidence.

Product should not keep engineers away from users, from ambiguity, or from the problem frame.

And product should not define its value by how much gets added.

That last point matters more than most organisations want to admit.

A product function that cannot stop work is not leading product.
It is managing accumulation.

## Engineering should not hide behind neutrality

The correction cannot all flow in one direction.

Engineering should not behave as if its responsibility starts only after a specification is complete. Not if the team claims to be doing serious product work. If feasibility is central to discovery, engineering cannot opt out of problem framing without weakening the product itself.

Engineering should not talk about technical choices as though they lived outside business reality either. Technical and business decisions can be discussed through the same economic variables, including value, risk, cost, debt, and timing.

That shared language matters because it strips away a lot of fake conflict.

A technical concern is not “just engineering being difficult” when it is really a warning about future cost, future fragility, or future speed.
A product concern is not “just a stakeholder request” when it is really a question about value, timing, and opportunity cost.

Same product.
Same investment.
Different lens.

That does not remove disagreement.
It makes the disagreement worth having.

## Small steps are not a style preference

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!oRaY!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fca037ebd-cfbb-465d-9264-59ced937dbdf_1024x559.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!oRaY!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fca037ebd-cfbb-465d-9264-59ced937dbdf_1024x559.png)

One of the easier mistakes in this topic is to talk about small batches, feedback loops, and restraint as if they were culture slogans.

It ties them to sustainability.

The core idea is simple: a product is not static, the codebase is not free, and the team’s capacity is not infinite. Every addition has a carrying cost. So the collaboration between product and engineering cannot be judged only by output. It also has to be judged by whether it preserves the ability to keep making good changes later.

That is why small steps matter here.

Not because small is virtuous.
Because smaller bets make learning cheaper.
Because smaller bets reduce the blast radius of being wrong.
Because smaller bets make it easier to change your mind before the system hardens around a mistake.

That does not prove a universal law.
It does make one thing harder to deny:

A large plan can feel safe long before it earns the right to feel safe.

## The shared language should be economic, not tribal

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!IK5S!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe5cf00f6-da08-4e65-b5ac-7ec7d2e95a13_1024x559.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!IK5S!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe5cf00f6-da08-4e65-b5ac-7ec7d2e95a13_1024x559.png)

A lot of product-engineering conflict survives because each side speaks in a dialect that flatters its own concerns.

One side says growth, opportunity, customer need, and urgency.
The other says architecture, debt, scalability, and quality.

That is not wrong. It is just incomplete.

A better language cuts across both sides: value, risk, cost, debt, and timing.

This is one of the article’s most useful reframings.

Product is not defending “the business” against engineering.
Engineering is not defending “quality” against product.

Both are making claims about the same asset.
They are just closer to different failure modes.

Once you see that, the discussion improves.

“What mix of delivery, learning, risk reduction, and design investment best protects this product’s future?”

That is a serious question.
Departmental arguments usually are not.

## What a serious collaboration should actually look like

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!qDL7!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa273e8be-7a9a-4b67-8ac4-b1b0654a9c98_1024x558.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!qDL7!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa273e8be-7a9a-4b67-8ac4-b1b0654a9c98_1024x558.png)

A serious collaboration between product and engineering should not look like a neat handoff between two clean stages.

It should look like shared judgment with different responsibilities.

Product should answer:
What are we doing?
Why are we doing it?
What is it for?
How does it fit the strategy?
What does it add to the product?
What does it add to the company?
What should happen first, given the outcomes we want, the return we expect, and the direction we claim to be following?

Engineering should answer:
How do we build this safely?
How do we reduce technical risk?
How do we preserve adaptability?
How do we avoid spending future capacity too early?
How do we sequence the work responsibly?

And both should stay inside the harder question underneath all of that:

Does this bet deserve commitment now, later, or never?

That is the collaboration that matters.

Not hierarchy.
Not document relay.
Not the performance of alignment.

Shared judgment under uncertainty.

## The caveat that keeps the argument honest

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!idRl!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fba0b9bca-cfe3-48ff-85f9-52225cc8747f_1024x558.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!idRl!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fba0b9bca-cfe3-48ff-85f9-52225cc8747f_1024x558.png)

The support here is strongest as practitioner guidance, first-hand operational testimony, and conceptual framing. It is not a body of peer-reviewed causal research proving one universal model of product organisation.

That matters.

So the conclusion should stay narrower than the rhetoric many articles use.

This article is not claiming that every organisation must adopt one exact operating model.
It is not claiming that all roadmaps are useless.
It is not claiming that product has one fixed shape across all contexts.

It is making a more disciplined argument.

If product is reduced to backlog administration, the role becomes too small for the work it is supposed to do.
If engineering is reduced to downstream delivery, the team loses one of the functions it most needs during uncertainty.
And if product and engineering do not develop a shared language around value, risk, cost, debt, and timing, they will keep having shallow arguments about deeper problems.

## Conclusions

Product is not the backlog.

It is not the role that tidies requests before engineers see them.
It is not the polished name for project administration.
It is not the function that protects the organisation from uncertainty by converting uncertainty into tickets.

Product should answer the why, the what, and the strategic how.
Engineering should answer the technical how.

Product should help decide what deserves to exist.
Engineering should help decide what can exist safely, sustainably, and without wasting future capacity.

And both sides should stay close to the question that matters more than any roadmap, any spec, or any estimation ritual:

Is this actually the right bet?
