> A Tech Lead helps the team protect the software investment, manage uncertainty, and use limited capacity to obtain the highest sustainable Return on Investment (ROI).

That is the core idea of this model.

A Tech Lead is not just the most technical person on the team. They are not the person who reviews everything, decides everything, unblocks everything, or protects the team from Product.

A Tech Lead helps the team make better technical, operational, and organizational decisions under real constraints: capacity, risk, knowledge, time, cost, uncertainty, and coordination.

The job is not to maximize output. It is not to maximize throughput without looking at real value either. The job is to help the team make sure its throughput produces valuable outcomes without destroying the future capacity to keep changing the product.

> A good Tech Lead protects the team’s future ability to keep creating value.

A company invests money, time, attention, opportunity, and trust in a team.

The team turns that investment into software. But software is not the final goal.Software creates capability. Capability should create value. Value should create a return of investment. That return should justify the investment. The problem is that software also consumes capacity and at the same time is a cost.

Every live feature has to be understood, maintained, operated, tested, monitored, evolved, documented, and supported. Every technical decision increases or reduces the team’s future capacity. Every dependency can increase or reduce liquidity. Every knowledge silo can become risk. Every alert can protect the system or consume attention. Every new live system can increase the fixed cost the team has to carry.

And we make all of these decisions under uncertainty.

We do not know with certainty what users will do. We do not know which assumptions will fail. We do not know which technical decisions will become expensive later. We do not know which dependencies will slow us down. We do not know which features will justify their fixed cost. We do not know which risks will materialize.

That is why the Tech Lead’s work is not only technical execution.

> The Tech Lead protects the investment by helping the team manage uncertainty before it becomes expensive.

The Tech Lead does not only ask:

> Can we build this?

The better questions are:

1.   **Is this the best use of our capacity right now?**

2.   **What uncertainty are we carrying?**

3.   **What evidence do we need before committing more?**

4.   **What return do we expect from this investment?**

5.   **What risk are we accepting to pursue that return?**

6.   **What cost will this add after it exists?**

7.   **What options are we closing?**

8.   **What options are we keeping open?**

9.   **How do we maximize return without destroying future capacity?**

That is why the Tech Lead works inside a constant tension:

> Create new capability without consuming so much future capacity that the team stops being able to create value.

If the team only builds, the system grows until it becomes heavy.

If the team only cleans up, it stops delivering new value.

If the team only runs, it learns too late.

If the team only optimizes architecture, it may arrive too late to the market.

If the team only reacts to urgent work, it loses direction.

If the team only follows the roadmap, it may ignore the real cost of maintaining what already exists.

The Tech Lead helps the team hold that tension without turning it into a fight between Product and Technology.

A company does not pay a software team to produce code. It invests in the team because it expects a return.

That return may come through:

1.   **Revenue.**

2.   **Retention.**

3.   **Operational efficiency.**

4.   **Reduced risk.**

5.   **Faster learning.**

6.   **Lower support cost.**

7.   **Better customer experience.**

8.   **Stronger delivery capability.**

9.   **Increased strategic flexibility.**

10.   **Reduced software fixed cost.**

11.   **Improved ability to change direction.**

The Tech Lead helps protect that investment.

Protecting the investment does not mean spending as little as possible. It means spending capacity with intent.

It means asking whether the team is investing in the right thing, at the right time, with the right level of evidence, with an acceptable level of risk, and without damaging the team’s future ability to change.

Useful formula:

`Return on Investment (ROI) = (Benefit - Investment) / Investment`
But Return on Investment (ROI) is not enough by itself.

A high theoretical return can still be a bad decision if:

1.   **The evidence is weak.**

2.   **The risk is too high.**

3.   **The decision is hard to reverse.**

4.   **The cost of delay is misunderstood.**

5.   **The fixed cost after delivery is ignored.**

6.   **The work blocks better alternatives.**

7.   **The team loses liquidity.**

8.   **The outcome depends on too many teams finishing separate parts.**

9.   **The work increases Keep the Lights On (KTLO) beyond what the team can sustain.**

So the Tech Lead does not protect Return on Investment (ROI) by helping the team build more. The Tech Lead protects Return on Investment (ROI) by helping the team invest better.

A Tech Lead who solves everything difficult may look valuable, but they may be creating a system that depends on them.

Economic problem:

`Repeated heroics = Low bus factor + Blocked learning + Reduced liquidity`
If everything has to go through the Tech Lead, the team loses autonomy, judgment, and learning speed.

Economic problem:

`Centralized control = Lower throughput + Lower autonomy + Higher dependency`
Designing solutions far away from the real flow of work can produce architecture that looks elegant but does not help delivery.

Economic problem:

`Architecture without delivery = Technical decision without economic validation`
Putting out fires may be necessary. Living in firefighting mode without changing the system that creates the fires is a failure signal.

Economic problem:

`Recurring incident without improvement = Keep the Lights On (KTLO) becoming normal`
It is not enough to turn requests into technical tickets. The Tech Lead must help discuss value, cost, risk, sustainability, uncertainty, and capacity.

Economic problem:

`Output without judgment = Capacity invested without value protection`
A good technical practice can be a bad investment if it does not fit the context, time horizon, or real risk.

Economic problem:

`Good practice without trade-off = Weak argument`
Improving a part that does not constrain the system can create the feeling of progress without releasing real value.

Economic problem:

`Optimization outside the constraint = Local improvement with low system impact`
Accepting more software without reviewing what already exists increases fixed cost and reduces future capacity.

Economic problem:

`More software without retirement = Growing fixed cost + Shrinking future capacity`
> A Tech Lead protects the software investment by helping the team manage uncertainty, make better trade-offs, and use limited capacity to maximize sustainable Return on Investment (ROI).

This implies three responsibilities.

The Tech Lead helps the team reason better about value, evidence, uncertainty, cost, risk, time, knowledge, operations, and organization.

The Tech Lead watches whether today’s decisions are damaging tomorrow’s ability to change.

The Tech Lead does not sell decisions as if they were free. They explain what we gain, what we sacrifice, who pays the cost, and when that cost will be paid.

A supporting principle is:

> A Tech Lead does not exist to make every technical decision. A Tech Lead exists to improve the quality of the technical decisions the team makes.

This model does not assume the reader already understands the terms. It needs a shared vocabulary.

An investment is capacity spent now with the expectation of future benefit.

In software, investment is not only money.

It includes:

1.   **Team time.**

2.   **Attention.**

3.   **Knowledge.**

4.   **Coordination.**

5.   **Risk taken.**

6.   **Opportunity cost.**

7.   **Operational energy.**

8.   **Learning capacity.**

9.   **Future flexibility.**

When a team accepts a feature, migration, integration, dependency, abstraction, or debt, it is investing capacity.

Useful question:

> What benefit do we expect, when will it appear, how risky is it, and what future capacity are we committing?

Return on Investment (ROI) is a way to compare the benefit of an investment with the investment required.

Formula:

`Return on Investment (ROI) = (Benefit - Investment) / Investment`
In software, this is useful but incomplete.

The “investment” is not only implementation effort. It also includes the cost of maintenance, support, operations, coordination, cognitive load, risk, and opportunity cost.

The “benefit” is not only revenue. It can also be reduced risk, faster learning, reduced support, better flow, stronger delivery capability, or lower future cost.

Useful question:

> What return do we expect, how confident are we, and what total cost are we accepting to pursue it?

Uncertainty is the gap between what we need to know and what we actually know when making a decision.

Software work is full of uncertainty:

1.   **Product uncertainty:** Will users care?

2.   **Business uncertainty:** Will this create enough return?

3.   **Technical uncertainty:** Can we build and operate this safely?

4.   **Operational uncertainty:** What will it cost to keep alive?

5.   **Organizational uncertainty:** Can the required teams coordinate effectively?

6.   **Knowledge uncertainty:** Does the team understand enough to change this safely?

7.   **Timing uncertainty:** Will the value still matter when we deliver?

8.   **Economic uncertainty:** Will the return justify the total cost?

The Tech Lead does not remove uncertainty completely. That is impossible.

The Tech Lead helps the team manage uncertainty economically.

That means:

1.   **Buying information before large commitments.**

2.   **Keeping decisions reversible where possible.**

3.   **Reducing the impact of being wrong.**

4.   **Delivering smaller slices to learn earlier.**

5.   **Making assumptions explicit.**

6.   **Using evidence instead of pressure or opinion.**

7.   **Reducing risk before it becomes expensive.**

8.   **Avoiding large fixed costs before value is validated.**

Useful formulas:

`Economic decision quality ≈ Expected return × Confidence / Risk and cost of being wrong``Investment quality ≈ Expected outcome value × Confidence / Total cost, risk, and loss of flexibility`
Capacity is the team’s real ability to turn attention, knowledge, and time into safe, valuable change.

It is not just available hours.

Capacity depends on:

1.   **Available time.**

2.   **Priority clarity.**

3.   **Shared knowledge.**

4.   **Technical health.**

5.   **Operational health.**

6.   **Interruptions.**

7.   **Dependencies across teams.**

8.   **Tooling and automation.**

9.   **Cognitive load.**

10.   **Confidence to change the system.**

11.   **Fixed cost already committed.**

Useful formula:

`Capacity available for new value ≈ Total capacity - Software fixed cost - Keep the Lights On (KTLO) - Incidents - Coordination - Unplanned work`
Two teams with the same number of people do not have the same capacity if one of them is trapped in Keep the Lights On (KTLO), noisy alerts, fragile deployments, knowledge silos, and external dependencies.

Value is a meaningful improvement in reality.

It is not the same as producing something.

Examples of value:

1.   **Users complete onboarding faster.**

2.   **Customers pay with less friction.**

3.   **Support receives fewer incidents.**

4.   **Diagnosis time goes down.**

5.   **Conversion improves.**

6.   **Error rate drops.**

7.   **The team can deploy with less coordination.**

8.   **Critical knowledge stops being concentrated in one person.**

9.   **The company reduces legal, operational, or security exposure.**

Useful formula:

`Net value ≈ Benefit of the outcome - Build cost - Maintenance cost`
Useful question:

> What improved because of what we delivered?

Output is what the team produces.

Examples:

1.   **A feature delivered.**

2.   **An Application Programming Interface (API) published.**

3.   **A migration executed.**

4.   **A refactoring completed.**

5.   **A dashboard created.**

6.   **Documentation written.**

7.   **Automation added.**

8.   **A bug fixed.**

9.   **A release deployed.**

10.   **A number of tickets closed.**

Output is necessary, but output alone does not prove value was created.

A team can close many tickets and still improve nothing important.

Outcome is the real change caused by the output.

Examples:

1.   **Users abandon the sign-up flow less often.**

2.   **Customers complete purchases with fewer errors.**

3.   **Support receives fewer requests about the same issue.**

4.   **The team diagnoses incidents faster.**

5.   **The company reduces compliance risk.**

6.   **The team releases changes with less fear.**

7.   **A new joiner onboards faster.**

8.   **A critical area no longer depends on one person.**

9.   **A manual operation disappears.**

10.   **A feature that did not justify its cost is removed and frees capacity.**

Outcome matters more than output because it connects delivery to real improvement.

Throughput is the amount of completed work per unit of time.

It can be measured as completed items, deployed changes, released features, resolved incidents, or any relevant unit of finished work.

Throughput is not value. It is a flow metric.

High throughput can be good if the completed work produces valuable outcomes. But high throughput can be noise if the team is only closing low-value tickets, increasing fixed cost, generating more Keep the Lights On (KTLO), or fragmenting the system.

> High throughput without valuable outcomes is efficient waste.

Useful formulas:

*   Output = What we produce

*   Outcome = What improves in reality

*   Throughput = Completed work per unit of time

`Outcome value ≠ Output volume``Flow quality ≈ Valuable outcomes delivered / Total completed work`
Useful question:

> What throughput of valuable outputs can we sustain without increasing risk, fixed cost, Keep the Lights On (KTLO), cognitive load, or loss of liquidity?

Cost is the capacity consumed by a decision.

There are several types of cost.

Build cost is the cost of analyzing, designing, implementing, testing, deploying, and coordinating something.

Maintenance cost is the cost of keeping what we built alive.

It includes maintenance, support, monitoring, tests, updates, coordination, and cognitive load.

Useful formula:

`Total cost ≈ Build cost + Maintenance cost`

Software fixed cost is the capacity a system consumes simply because it exists and must continue to run, be understood, be operated, be updated, be protected, and be changed safely.

It includes:

1.   **Maintenance.**

2.   **Base support.**

3.   **Monitoring.**

4.   **Regression testing.**

5.   **Dependency updates.**

6.   **Security patches.**

7.   **Documentation.**

8.   **Operations.**

9.   **Coordination.**

10.   **Cognitive load.**

11.   **Ownership.**

12.   **On-call responsibilities.**

13.   **Alert review.**

14.   **Backward compatibility.**

> A feature does not only cost money when it is built. It also increases the fixed cost base the team has to carry.

Useful formula:

`Software fixed cost ≈ Maintenance + Base support + Monitoring + Tests + Updates + Coordination + Cognitive load`
Variable cost increases with usage, traffic, data, customers, or operations.

Examples:

1.   **Infrastructure proportional to usage.**

2.   **Storage.**

3.   **Processing.**

4.   **Calls to external services.**

5.   **Cost per transaction.**

6.   **Support proportional to the number of users.**

Useful formula:

`Variable cost ≈ Cost per unit of usage × Usage volume`
Step fixed cost looks fixed within a range, but increases when the system crosses certain thresholds.

Examples:

1.   **More users require on-call coverage.**

2.   **More criticality requires better observability.**

3.   **More integrations require more formal ownership.**

4.   **More volume requires infrastructure redesign.**

5.   **More regulation requires additional controls.**

6.   **More teams consuming an Application Programming Interface (API) require stricter backward compatibility.**

7.   **More enterprise customers require support, Service Level Agreements (SLAs), and audits.**

Useful formula:

`Step fixed cost ≈ New operating level required after crossing a threshold of usage, criticality, complexity, or dependency`
> More software can create more capability, but it also increases fixed cost, change cost, cognitive load, and operational responsibility.

Run cost is the cost of keeping the system working.

It includes Keep the Lights On (KTLO), support, infrastructure, on-call work, incidents, alerts, monitoring, and maintenance.

Change cost is the cost of modifying the system safely.

It includes understanding, testing, coordination, deployment, migration, validation, and rollback.

This distinction matters because some decisions reduce run cost, others reduce change cost, and some reduce one while increasing the other.

Keep the Lights On (KTLO) is all the work required to keep the system running.

It includes:

1.   **Support.**

2.   **Incidents.**

3.   **On-call work.**

4.   **Maintenance.**

5.   **Dependency updates.**

6.   **Security patches.**

7.   **Infrastructure management.**

8.   **Monitoring.**

9.   **Alert review.**

10.   **Recurring manual tasks.**

11.   **Small fixes.**

12.   **Unplanned operational work.**

13.   **Recurring operational coordination.**

Keep the Lights On (KTLO) is not bad by default. Every live system needs maintenance. The problem appears when Keep the Lights On (KTLO) grows without visibility and starts consuming the capacity that planning assumed was available for new value.

Useful formulas:

`Keep the Lights On ratio (KTLO ratio) ≈ Capacity spent keeping the existing system alive / Total team capacity``Avoidable Keep the Lights On (Avoidable KTLO) ≈ Total Keep the Lights On (Total KTLO) - Healthy Keep the Lights On (Healthy KTLO)`
Useful question:

> What part of Keep the Lights On (KTLO) is healthy, and what part is capacity trapped by complexity, fragility, lack of automation, lack of observability, excessive coordination, or too much software?

Risk is the combination of the probability of a harmful result and the impact of that result.

Useful formula:

`Risk ≈ Probability of harmful outcome × Impact of being wrong`
The impact of being wrong is not always the same.

Being wrong about button copy does not cost the same as being wrong about the data model, authorization, billing, security, architecture, or a critical migration.

Useful formulas:

`Impact of being wrong ≈ Sunk implementation cost + Rollback or migration cost + Disruption cost + Delay introduced + Opportunity cost``Disruption cost ≈ Blast radius × Duration of exposure`
Useful question:

> What happens if we are wrong?

Sunk cost is cost already paid that cannot be recovered.

In software, it can be analysis, design, implementation, coordination, testing, or deployment time already consumed.

Sunk cost should be understood, but it should not justify continuing with a bad decision.

Useful question:

> If we started today with what we know now, would we make this decision again?

Liquidity is the ability to change direction without losing too much value, time, knowledge, or energy.

A liquid team can move.

An illiquid team gets trapped.

Liquidity goes down when:

1.   **There is too much Work in Progress (WIP).**

2.   **Changes are large.**

3.   **Dependencies across teams are strong.**

4.   **Knowledge is concentrated.**

5.   **Deployments are fragile.**

6.   **Every release needs heavy coordination.**

7.   **The system is hard to understand.**

8.   **Decisions are hard to reverse.**

9.   **The team cannot operate what it builds.**

10.   **A customer-facing feature requires too many teams.**

Useful formula:

`Liquidity ≈ Ability to change direction / Cost of changing direction`
Useful question:

> Will we be able to change direction after this decision?

Opportunity cost is the value of the best alternative we are not pursuing because we chose this path.

Useful formula:

`Opportunity cost ≈ Value of the best alternative not pursued`
Useful question:

> What are we not doing because we chose this?

Cost of delay is the value lost while something valuable is not available.

Useful formula:

`Cost of delay ≈ Value lost per period × Duration of delay`
It helps separate importance from urgency.

Something can be valuable without being urgent.

Something can have modest total value but lose a lot of value if it arrives late.

Useful question:

> What do we lose if we wait?

Amortization is the time an investment needs to pay back its cost through repeated benefits.

Useful formula:

`Time to amortize ≈ Investment cost / Net benefit per period`
Example:

```
Time to amortize ≈ 10 days of investment / 1 day saved per month
Time to amortize ≈ 10 months
```

This helps decide whether investment in automation, refactoring, observability, testing, pipelines, documentation, or operational load reduction is justified.

Useful question:

> Will we use this often enough, for long enough, to pay back the cost with reasonable confidence?

Reversibility is how easily a decision can be undone, modified, or replaced.

A reversible decision lets us learn with less fear.

An irreversible decision requires more care, more evidence, and better risk management.

Useful formula:

`Decision danger ≈ Impact of being wrong × Irreversibility × Weak evidence`
Useful question:

> Can we change direction if this does not work?

Cognitive load is the mental effort needed to understand, predict, coordinate, operate, and safely change the system.

It does not live only in the code.

It also appears in alerts, processes, cross-team coordination, environments, deployments, dependencies, permissions, handoffs, and knowledge distribution.

Useful formula:

`Cognitive cost ≈ Time needed to understand enough context to make a safe change`
Common sources of cognitive load:

1.   **Code that is hard to understand.**

2.   **Business domain that is not explicit.**

3.   **Architecture with too many hidden relationships.**

4.   **Dependencies across teams.**

5.   **Difficult deployment processes.**

6.   **Excessive or non-actionable alerts.**

7.   **Recurring incidents without learning.**

8.   **Outdated documentation.**

9.   **Tests that do not explain behavior.**

10.   **Scattered configuration.**

11.   **Permissions that are hard to understand.**

12.   **Unclear ownership.**

13.   **Shared systems without a real owner.**

14.   **Frequent handoffs.**

15.   **The need to coordinate several teams to deliver customer value.**

[![Image 9](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!rDF1!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F50c68027-1734-46f2-bfd9-5ad7a749fc93_1024x572.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!rDF1!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F50c68027-1734-46f2-bfd9-5ad7a749fc93_1024x572.png)

Alert fatigue appears when the team receives too many alerts, alerts that are not actionable, or alerts without a clear connection to a decision.

The problem is not only interruption. It is cognitive load and loss of sensitivity.

> If everything alerts, nothing alerts.

A useful alert should help someone decide.

Useful formulas:

`Alert fatigue risk ≈ Alert volume × Noise ratio × Interruption cost × Lack of actionability``Alert value ≈ Actionability × Risk reduction / Interruption cost`
Useful questions:

1.   **Does this alert require a clear action?**

2.   **Can the person receiving it act?**

3.   **Does the alert include enough context?**

4.   **Does the alert distinguish real urgency from noise?**

5.   **Does the alert repeat without improving the system?**

6.   **Does the alert interrupt more than it protects?**

Coordination across teams also consumes mental capacity.

When a customer-facing feature needs several teams, the work is not only divided. Context is fragmented.

Each team understands a part. Nobody sees the full flow. Waiting, handoffs, meetings, dependencies, different interpretations, priority changes, and loss of ownership appear.

Useful formula:

`Coordination cognitive load ≈ Number of teams × Handoffs × Context gaps × Frequency of change`
Useful question:

> Are we paying cognitive load because the system is complex, or because the organization makes value delivery unnecessarily complex?

Technical debt is future burden deliberately accepted to gain value sooner, with a repayment plan.

It is not any bad code.

It is not neglect.

It is not lack of investment.

Useful question:

> What are we borrowing from the future, what do we gain now, and when will we pay it back?

Cognitive debt is the gap between the system’s behavior and the team’s shared understanding.

Examples:

1.   **The code works, but nobody knows why.**

2.   **The architecture contains old decisions nobody remembers.**

3.   **One person knows business rules that are not in tests or documentation.**

4.   **Teams operate alerts they do not understand.**

5.   **Changes require informal coordination because boundaries are unclear.**

6.   **Incidents are resolved through tribal memory.**

Operational debt is future burden accepted in operations.

Examples:

1.   **Temporary manual processes.**

2.   **Imperfect alerts.**

3.   **Incomplete runbooks.**

4.   **On-call work dependent on tribal knowledge.**

5.   **Operational patches without automation.**

Useful question:

> What operational work are we accepting today, and what is the plan to reduce it?

Organizational debt is future cost accepted by keeping team boundaries, dependencies, or handoffs that we know make delivery more expensive.

Useful question:

> What coordination cost are we accepting by not changing ownership, team structure, or architectural boundaries?

Neglect is cost created by not caring for the system and not making conscious decisions.

It should not be called debt.

Useful question:

> Was this a deliberate decision, or did we just let the system degrade?

Lack of investment is a problem that exists because enough capacity was never dedicated to a real need.

Useful question:

> Does this problem exist because we made a bad decision, or because we never invested enough?

Bus factor is the minimum number of people who would have to disappear from the project for the project to stop because of missing knowledge or capability.

In this model, bus factor is not a people metric. It is a risk metric.

Useful formula:

`Bus factor risk ≈ Criticality of area / Number of people who can safely change the area`
Useful question:

> What happens if the person who knows this area best is unavailable?

A knowledge silo exists when important knowledge is trapped in a person, team, role, or department.

The problem is not the person. The problem is that the system depends too much on knowledge that does not circulate.

Useful formula:

`Knowledge risk ≈ Criticality of area × Degree of knowledge concentration`

Conway’s law says that organizations tend to design systems that mirror their communication structures.

In practical terms: if a company is divided into silos, with many handoffs, heavy coordination, and rigid boundaries, its architecture will tend to reflect those same boundaries.

Work in Progress (WIP) is work that has started but is not finished.

The Tech Lead must separate low-risk decisions from high-risk decisions.

> Will we be able to change direction afterward?

Liquidity measures the ability to move.

A liquid team is not just a fast team. It is a team that can redirect capacity without losing too much time, knowledge, or value.

The liquidity lens exists because Return on Investment (ROI) is not only about what comes back. It is also about how much freedom remains after committing.

> Does the team structure help or make delivery more expensive?

Architecture and organization influence each other.

A Tech Lead must know when a technical problem is actually a sociotechnical problem.

If delivering a feature requires separate frontend, backend, Quality Assurance (QA), operations, data, security, and architecture groups, the problem may not be only in the code.

Ownership may be poorly designed.

The architecture may reflect a fragmented organization.

The organization may be imposing coordination where autonomy should exist.

Something subtler can also happen. The organization may have teams that look autonomous. Each team may have clear ownership over one part of the system. But creating real value for the final user may still require three or more teams to collaborate.

12.   **Measure progress by outcome unblocked, not local output completed.**

13.   **Create earlier integration mechanisms across teams.**

14.   **Review whether declared team autonomy actually produces delivery autonomy.**

The problem is not that several teams collaborate. That can be necessary and reasonable.

The problem appears when the organization calls something autonomy even though, in practice, it does not allow autonomous value delivery.

A team can be autonomous over its repository, service, or component, and still not be autonomous over the outcome. If final user value depends on other teams finishing their parts, real autonomy is limited.

> The autonomy that matters is not only autonomy over components. It is autonomy to deliver, operate, and learn from real value.

This distinction matters for a Tech Lead because it avoids a common misunderstanding: assuming the problem is that teams are “slow,” when the real issue may be that the work is sliced in a way that prevents value from flowing.

> How much capacity does it consume to keep the system running?

Operations are not an afterthought.

A system that requires too much care consumes capacity every week.

Keep the Lights On (KTLO) must be visible in planning. If it is not measured and discussed, the roadmap will assume capacity that does not really exist.

The operations lens exists because an investment can only produce sustainable return if the team can operate the result without exhausting future capacity.

2.   **Which operational tasks are unavoidable?**

A Tech Lead should not use one tool for everything.

Must have, Should have, Could have, Won’t have (MoSCoW), Reach, Impact, Confidence, Effort (RICE), and the Tech Lead economic rubric answer different questions.

Must have, Should have, Could have, Won’t have (MoSCoW) decides what goes into a delivery.

Reach, Impact, Confidence, Effort (RICE) helps order product opportunities.

The Tech Lead economic rubric decides where to invest limited technical capacity when value, risk, Keep the Lights On (KTLO), knowledge, liquidity, organization, and future cost compete.

Must have, Should have, Could have, Won’t have (MoSCoW) is useful for scope decisions.

Question it answers:

> What must go into this delivery?

Categories:

1.   **Must have:** Without this, the delivery does not fulfill its purpose.

2.   **Should have:** Important, but the delivery can survive without it.

3.   **Could have:** Valuable, but not essential now.

4.   **Won’t have:** Not part of this delivery or decision cycle.

Something should only be Must have if it meets one of these conditions:

1.   **Without it, there is no minimum outcome.**

2.   **Without it, legal, operational, security, or contractual risk is unacceptable.**

3.   **Without it, the delivery cannot be used in a real way.**

4.   **Without it, the main hypothesis cannot be validated.**

Something should be Should have if:

1.   **It clearly improves the outcome.**

2.   **It reduces important friction.**

3.   **It reduces risk, but does not block the delivery.**

4.   **It can be deferred without destroying the main value.**

Something should be Could have if:

1.   **It provides a secondary improvement.**

2.   **It does not change whether the delivery is valid.**

3.   **It does not reduce critical risk.**

4.   **It should only happen if real capacity exists.**

Something should be Won’t have if:

1.   **It is not needed for the current outcome.**

2.   **There is not enough evidence.**

3.   **It adds too much fixed cost.**

4.   **It increases dependencies or Keep the Lights On (KTLO).**

5.   **It can wait without meaningful cost of delay.**

Warning:

> An initiative should not become Must have if the system cannot sustain it safely.

Reach, Impact, Confidence, Effort (RICE) is useful for ordering product opportunities.

Question it answers:

> Which product opportunity should go first?

Components:

1.   **Reach:** How many people, customers, users, operations, or cases it affects.

2.   **Impact:** How much impact it will have if it works.

3.   **Confidence:** How confident we are in the estimate.

4.   **Effort:** How much effort it requires.

Formula:

`Reach, Impact, Confidence, Effort (RICE) = (Reach × Impact × Confidence) / Effort`
Reach, Impact, Confidence, Effort (RICE) does not capture well on its own:

1.   **Bus factor.**

2.   **Knowledge silos.**

3.   **Keep the Lights On (KTLO).**

4.   **Conway’s law.**

5.   **Fixed cost.**

6.   **Cognitive load.**

7.   **Alert fatigue.**

8.   **Cross-team coordination.**

9.   **Reversibility.**

10.   **Operational risk.**

11.   **Future cost.**

12.   **Capacity lost to maintenance.**

Sustainability check:

`Sustainability-adjusted Reach, Impact, Confidence, Effort (Sustainability-adjusted RICE) ≈ Reach, Impact, Confidence, Effort (RICE) / (Fixed cost added + Operational risk added + Dependencies added)`
Useful question:

> Even if Reach, Impact, Confidence, Effort (RICE) is high, what fixed cost, Keep the Lights On (KTLO), operational risk, or organizational dependency are we adding?

The economic rubric helps compare mixed decisions.

Question it answers:

> Where should we invest limited technical capacity?

It helps compare decisions of different types:

1.   **Build a feature.**

2.   **Reduce bus factor.**

3.   **Automate Keep the Lights On (KTLO).**

4.   **Add observability.**

5.   **Remove a legacy feature.**

6.   **Reduce dependencies across teams.**

7.   **Refactor a high-change area.**

8.   **Update dependencies.**

9.   **Slice an initiative that is too large.**

10.   **Buy information before building.**

11.   **Reduce alert fatigue.**

12.   **Reorganize ownership to deliver end-to-end value.**

`Priority ≈ Expected economic benefit / Total decision cost`
Where:

`Expected economic benefit = Value + Risk reduction + Cost reduction + Liquidity improvement + Knowledge improvement`
And:

`Total decision cost = Effort + Fixed cost added + Variable cost added + Maintenance cost added + Cognitive load added + Dependencies added`
Score each criterion from 1 to 5.

Scale:

1.   **1 means low, irrelevant, or unclear.**

2.   **2 means somewhat relevant.**

3.   **3 means relevant.**

4.   **4 means high.**

5.   **5 means critical.**

Positive criteria:

1.   **Potential value:** How much value it can create or protect.

2.   **Expected return:** How much return the investment may produce.

3.   **Cost of delay:** How much we lose if we wait.

4.   **Current risk:** What exposure exists if we do nothing.

5.   **Fixed cost reduction:** How much future capacity it frees.

6.   **Keep the Lights On (KTLO) reduction:** How much recurring operational work it reduces.

7.   **Liquidity improvement:** How much it helps us change direction.

8.   **Conway risk reduction:** How much it reduces dependencies and coordination across teams.

9.   **Knowledge risk reduction:** How much it reduces bus factor, silos, or cognitive load.

10.   **Operational fatigue reduction:** How much it reduces noise, interruptions, useless alerts, or support load.

11.   **Outcome measurement improvement:** How much it helps us know whether output produces value.

12.   **Uncertainty reduction:** How much it helps us learn before committing more.

13.   **Reversibility:** How easy it is to change direction afterward.

14.   **Confidence:** What evidence we have about the benefit.

Cost criteria:

1.   **Effort:** How much capacity it consumes.

2.   **Fixed cost added:** How much permanent load it adds.

3.   **Variable cost added:** How much it may grow with usage.

4.   **Maintenance cost added:** How much Keep the Lights On (KTLO) or maintenance it adds.

5.   **Cognitive load added:** How much extra understanding, coordination, or attention it requires.

6.   **Dependencies added:** How much new coordination it requires with other teams.

7.   **Loss of flexibility:** How much it reduces future options.

Formula:

```
Priority score =
(Potential value + Expected return + Cost of delay + Current risk + Fixed cost reduction + Keep the Lights On (KTLO) reduction + Liquidity improvement + Conway risk reduction + Knowledge risk reduction + Operational fatigue reduction + Outcome measurement improvement + Uncertainty reduction + Reversibility + Confidence)
/
(Effort + Fixed cost added + Variable cost added + Maintenance cost added + Cognitive load added + Dependencies added + Loss of flexibility)
```

This formula is not mathematical truth. It is a thinking tool.

The goal is not for the number to decide. The goal is for the team to discuss the right variables.

I would not call it a technical debt backlog.

I would call it a decision backlog.

It should contain any candidate investment of capacity:

1.   **New features.**

2.   **Technical risks.**

3.   **Operational risks.**

4.   **Knowledge risks.**

5.   **Recurring Keep the Lights On (KTLO).**

6.   **Delivery improvements.**

7.   **Software removal.**

8.   **Automation.**

9.   **Observability.**

10.   **Updates.**

11.   **Ownership changes.**

12.   **Refactorings.**

13.   **Spikes or prototypes.**

14.   **Alert fatigue reduction.**

15.   **Cross-team coordination reduction.**

16.   **Outcome measurement improvements.**

17.   **Fixed cost reduction.**

18.   **Uncertainty reduction.**

Useful types:

1.   **Value creation:** Work expected to create new value.

2.   **Return improvement:** Work expected to increase return from existing or future investment.

3.   **Risk reduction:** Work that reduces probability or impact of failure.

4.   **Cost reduction:** Work that reduces repeated effort, fixed cost, or change cost.

5.   **Keep the Lights On (KTLO) reduction:** Work that reduces recurring operational load.

6.   **Liquidity improvement:** Work that makes it easier to move.

7.   **Knowledge improvement:** Work that reduces silos, bus factor, or cognitive load.

8.   **Organizational improvement:** Work that reduces dependencies across teams or coordination cost.

9.   **Option preservation:** Work that avoids premature commitments.

10.   **Waste removal:** Work that removes software, process, or effort without enough value.

11.   **Information purchase:** Work that reduces uncertainty before commitment.

12.   **Operability improvement:** Work that reduces noisy alerts, improves diagnosis, or reduces manual intervention.

13.   **Measurement improvement:** Work that helps distinguish output, throughput, and outcome.

Rule:

*   If we are deciding scope, use Must have, Should have, Could have, Won’t have (MoSCoW).

*   If we are ordering product opportunities, use Reach, Impact, Confidence, Effort (RICE).

*   If we are allocating limited technical capacity across product, risk, Keep the Lights On (KTLO), knowledge, organization, liquidity, operations, and uncertainty, use the Tech Lead economic rubric.

Use the short rubric for fast conversations.

Use the extended rubric for relevant, expensive, irreversible, or systemic decisions.

Some decisions should move up in priority even if the score does not look spectacular.

Escalate a decision if any of these are true:

1.   **Bus factor of one in a critical area.**

2.   **High blast radius if it fails.**

3.   **No clear rollback.**

4.   **Weak observability in a critical area.**

5.   **Keep the Lights On (KTLO) growing for weeks or months.**

6.   **Recurring manual work consuming capacity.**

7.   **Outdated dependencies close to becoming a large migration.**

8.   **Security, privacy, billing, legal, or customer data is affected.**

9.   **Hard-to-reverse decision based on weak evidence.**

10.   **A frequent feature requires too many teams.**

11.   **The team cannot deploy or operate what it builds.**

12.   **The team is afraid to change a high-change area.**

13.   **More work is being opened because finishing is painful.**

14.   **A live feature consumes more capacity than the value it creates.**

15.   **The team has alert fatigue.**

16.   **Alerts are not actionable.**

17.   **We cannot measure whether output produces outcome.**

18.   **Throughput increases while real value does not improve.**

19.   **Fixed cost is growing without retirement, simplification, or automation.**

20.   **Several teams finish their parts, but the outcome remains blocked until other teams finish.**

21.   **The expected return depends on assumptions nobody has validated.**

The answer is not always “do it.”

Possible actions:

1.   **Build:** When value is clear and cost is acceptable.

2.   **Slice:** When there is value, but the batch is too large.

3.   **Learn:** When uncertainty is high.

4.   **Stabilize:** When operational risk is too high.

5.   **Simplify:** When fixed cost, change cost, or cognitive load is too high.

6.   **Share:** When knowledge is concentrated.

7.   **Remove:** When something consumes more capacity than the value it creates.

8.   **Automate:** When repeated Keep the Lights On (KTLO) consumes capacity.

9.   **Reorganize:** When team structure makes delivery more expensive.

10.   **Defer:** When there is not enough reason to commit now.

11.   **Reject:** When the investment is not justified.

12.   **Buy information:** When an important decision depends on reducible uncertainty.

13.   **Reduce noise:** When alerts, interruptions, or handoffs consume attention.

14.   **Measure outcome:** When we do not know whether output produces value.

15.   **Reconsider ownership:** When nobody can build, operate, learn from, and evolve the full capability.

16.   **Reconsider slicing:** When teams finish local parts, but end-to-end value remains blocked.

A Tech Lead must translate technical problems into economic language without emptying technical concepts of meaning.

> We need time for technical debt.

Say:

> This area is increasing the cost and risk of the next changes. The next roadmap items go through this part of the system, and right now the team does not trust the tests. If we invest before adding more behavior, we reduce regression probability and the cost of future changes.

> This architecture is bad.

Say:

> The current structure couples behaviors that change for different reasons. That is why small changes require more coordination and testing than they should. If this area continues to change, the current architecture will keep consuming capacity.

> We need more tests.

Say:

> Changing this behavior is expensive right now because we cannot detect regressions with confidence. Tests here would reduce the cost and risk of the next changes.

> Product changes priorities too much.

Say:

> Priorities change because new information appears. Our problem is that our work is in batches that are too large and expensive to pause. We need smaller slices so we can respond without losing so much capacity.

> Only Marta knows that.

Say:

> This area is critical and knowledge is concentrated in one person. That creates operational and delivery risk. We should pair on the next changes and preserve key behavior in tests or documentation.

> We have too much maintenance.

Say:

> A meaningful part of our capacity is being consumed by Keep the Lights On (KTLO). We need to separate healthy maintenance from avoidable Keep the Lights On (Avoidable KTLO), because the roadmap assumes capacity we do not actually have.

> We depend on too many teams.

Say:

> To deliver a small feature, we need to coordinate several teams. That increases waiting time, context loss, and coordination cost. We may have an ownership boundary problem, not only a technical problem.

> We have too many alerts.

Say:

> Alerts are consuming attention without generating clear action. That increases cognitive load and reduces our sensitivity to real problems. We need to distinguish actionable alerts from operational noise.

> We delivered a lot.

Say:

> We produced a lot of output. Now we need to review which outcomes it produced and whether the throughput we are optimizing is moving real value or only increasing activity.

> This feature is small.

Say:

> The build cost looks small, but we need to review what fixed cost, Keep the Lights On (KTLO), support, alerts, documentation, tests, and ownership it adds after deployment.

> My team already finished its part.

Say:

> We completed our local output, but the user-facing outcome is still blocked until the other parts are integrated, deployed, and validated. We need to manage this as a value-flow dependency, not as finished progress.

> This is technically the best option.

Say:

> This option may be technically strong, but we need to check whether it is the best investment under our current uncertainty, risk, time, and capacity constraints.

> We should build this.

Say:

> We should invest in this if the expected return, evidence, risk, and total cost make it stronger than the alternatives we are not choosing.

This model does not turn complex decisions into exact calculations.

It does not replace professional judgment.

It does not remove uncertainty.

It does not solve organizational conflict by itself.

It does not turn all value into money.

It does not make everything perfectly measurable.

It does not justify analysis paralysis.

It does not make all fixed cost bad.

It does not imply that all software should be removed because it has cost.

It does not say that collaboration across teams is bad. It says we need to understand when collaboration is a real domain need and when it is a signal that the work system blocks value flow.

It does not say that Return on Investment (ROI) is a perfect decision rule. It says that investment, return, uncertainty, risk, time, cost, and future capacity must be discussed together.

It exists to improve the conversation, make trade-offs visible, and help the team invest limited capacity better.

A Tech Lead protects the software investment.

To do that, they help the team manage uncertainty and use limited capacity to obtain the highest sustainable Return on Investment (ROI).

That does not mean maximizing short-term output.

It means balancing:

2.   **Investment required.**

3.   **Evidence available.**

4.   **Uncertainty carried.**

5.   **Risk of being wrong.**

6.   **Cost of delay.**

9.   **Cost of change.**

13.   **Future liquidity.**

The key question is not:

> How much can we build?

The better question is:

> How do we invest our limited capacity under uncertainty to create the highest sustainable return without damaging our ability to keep changing?

And the final sentence of the model is:

> A good Tech Lead does not make the team depend on their judgment. A good Tech Lead helps the team protect the investment, manage uncertainty, and make better decisions without depending on them.
